A lottery décimo is a bearer instrument: whoever physically holds it can claim the prize. So if you share one at work on nothing more than a verbal agreement or an ‘ok’ over WhatsApp and a prize then comes up, proving it belonged to several people can turn into a serious problem. It isn't about distrust: it's that, without proof, the law goes by the facts, not by good intentions.

The good news is that you don't need a notary or a complicated contract. A verbal agreement is valid too… as long as you can prove it existed. And a chat message or an email can serve as proof, but only if they meet certain specific conditions.

In this guide we go through the most common legal mistakes when splitting a décimo at the office and, above all, what a WhatsApp message or an email must contain to serve as solid supporting proof. An honest note: this is general information, not legal advice; for large prizes or tricky cases, consult a solicitor or tax adviser.

The key legal point

Without proof of the split, whoever holds the décimo calls the shots

Because the décimo is a bearer instrument, if the person keeping it claims the prize and refuses to share it, everyone else can only make a claim if they can prove it was shared. A verbal agreement counts, but you have to prove it; and keeping the prize from a shared décimo can amount to the offence of apropiación indebida (misappropriation). A WhatsApp message only works as proof if it identifies the décimo, who is taking part and for how much. The safest thing is to put it all in writing before the draw.

Why sharing ‘verbally’ is a real risk

 

The problem isn't trust, it's proof. While there's no prize, nobody argues; the dispute turns up precisely when the ticket wins and money is on the table. At that point, the law doesn't look at friendship, but at what can be proven.

As a bearer instrument, the décimo ‘belongs’, for the purpose of claiming the prize, to whoever presents it. If that person decides to keep everything, the rest will have to prove in court that there was an agreement to play it jointly. Case law is clear that a verbal sharing agreement is binding, but it requires proof that the agreement actually existed, and that is where most informal cases fall apart.

It's worth knowing because the outcome can be very serious: refusing to share the prize from a décimo that belonged to everyone can constitute the offence of apropiación indebida (misappropriation), with consequences that depend on the amount involved. It isn't just losing the money: it's the kind of mess nobody wants at Christmas or at any time of year.

The most common legal mistakes at the office

 

Almost all disputes come from the same slip-ups, and every one of them can be avoided with a little organisation. These are the mistakes that most often end in an argument (or in court):

✗Sharing only verbally: ‘we know where we stand’ is not proof if one day someone changes their story.

✗Sending just a photo of the décimo: it proves the number exists, but not who is taking part or for how much.

✗Relying on a stray ‘ok’: on its own it's weak proof; it needs to be backed up with details and supporting evidence.

✗Not identifying the décimo: without the number, series, fraction and draw, the message is worth little.

✗Leaving no date before the draw: the agreement has to come beforehand, not be an improvised split after the prize.

✗Not declaring joint ownership when claiming: it can lead Hacienda (the Spanish tax authority) to treat the split as a gift.

What a WhatsApp message or email needs to count as proof

 

A chat message or an email can be solid supporting proof if they leave no doubt about what is being played and who is taking part. These are the elements that can't be missing:

→ Identifying the décimo: the number (the five digits), the series, the fraction and the specific draw.

→ Who is taking part and for how much: each person's name and their share, in euros or as a percentage. It should add up to the total, with no ‘gaps’.

→ A date before the draw: it must be clear that the agreement came first, not a split decided after seeing there was a prize.

→ Confirmation from everyone: each participant should reply and accept their share in the same thread, not a one-sided message.

→ Extras that make it watertight: a traceable payment (a Bizum or a bank transfer with a reference) and, better still, a copy of the décimo signed by whoever is keeping it.

Not all proof is worth the same

 

The difference between winning or losing a claim comes down to the quality of the proof. Here's how differently each way of sharing holds up:

How strong each kind of proof is

How you share itValue as proof
Verbally only, nothing in writingVery weak: you have to prove the agreement existed
Just a photo of the décimo, nothing elseWeak: it doesn't say who is taking part or for how much
A stray ‘ok’ or a screenshotShaky on its own; better with other supporting evidence
A message with the number, participants and amountsSolid, especially with a traceable payment
A signed copy with everyone's DNI (ID) and amountsThe strongest

The authenticity of a message can be challenged: if someone claims it was tampered with, you'll have to prove it's genuine. That's why it's worth backing the proof up with payments and, ideally, a signed copy.

Watch out for Hacienda too. If a single person claims the shared prize and then splits it, the tax office may read it as a gift. To avoid this, identify all the participants at the moment of claiming (joint ownership), so that each one is taxed on their own share and you don't pay more than you should.

How to split a ticket without ending up in court

 

Making an office décimo watertight takes five minutes. With these steps, the prize reaches the winners in full, with no nasty surprises:

1

Put the agreement in writing before the draw

In the group chat or by email: number, series, fraction, draw, and each participant's name and amount (or percentage).

2

Have everyone confirm their share

Each person replies accepting their own share in the same thread. A one-sided agreement doesn't count; the deal belongs to everyone.

3

Leave a trail for the payment

Have each person pay in their share by Bizum or bank transfer, with a clear reference (‘Office Primitiva syndicate’). The payment backs up the agreement.

4

Keep a signed copy of the décimo

The strongest option: a photocopy of the décimo signed by whoever is looking after it, with the names, DNI and amounts. A photo on your phone helps too.

5

Declare joint ownership when you claim

If it wins, all of you should identify yourselves at the bank so the prize is recorded as shared and isn't treated as a gift.

Frequently asked questions about sharing décimos at work

 
Is a WhatsApp message enough to prove a décimo is shared?

It can be, but only if it contains the key details: identification of the décimo (number, series, fraction and draw), who is taking part and with what share, and a date before the draw. A stray ‘ok’ or a simple photo are weak proof on their own. On top of that, if the other party claims the message was tampered with, you'll have to prove it's genuine. That's why it's worth backing it up with traceable payments and, if possible, a signed copy of the décimo.

Do you need to go to a notary or sign a contract?

It isn't compulsory. A verbal agreement is valid, and you don't need a notarised document for the split to be enforceable. The problem isn't the form, it's the proof: you have to be able to show that the agreement existed before the draw. That's why we recommend putting it in writing (even in a chat with all the details) and backing it up with each person's payment. For very large prizes, a document signed by everyone is the safest option.

What happens if the person keeping the décimo keeps the prize?

Because the décimo is a bearer instrument, that person can claim it; but if it was shared and they refuse to split it, the rest can take legal action, and keeping what isn't theirs can constitute the offence of apropiación indebida (misappropriation), with consequences depending on the amount. The deciding factor will be the proof of the agreement: that's why it's so important to leave a written record, and a record of payment, before the draw.

Is a verbal agreement legally valid?

Yes. In Spain a verbal agreement to play and share a décimo jointly is valid and binding. The difficulty lies in proving it: without witnesses, messages or payments to back it up, it's very hard to establish in court, and many cases are lost precisely for lack of evidence. A verbal agreement backed by a complete chat and a trail of payments is far more defensible than a simple ‘we already talked about it’.

How do I stop Hacienda treating the split as a gift?

By declaring joint ownership at the moment of claiming: if the prize is large and you have to go through the bank, have all the participants identify themselves with their DNI, so that it's recorded as a shared prize from the outset and each one is taxed on their own share. If a single person claims it and then splits it ‘by hand’, Hacienda may treat it as a gift and demand Inheritance and Gift Tax.

What exactly should the message include to be good proof?

Five things: the décimo identified (number, series, fraction and draw), who is taking part and for how much (each person's amount or percentage, adding up to the total), a date before the draw, confirmation from everyone in the same thread and, as a backup, a traceable payment (a Bizum or a bank transfer with a reference) and a copy of the décimo signed by whoever is keeping it. With that, the message goes from being a memory to being solid supporting proof.
