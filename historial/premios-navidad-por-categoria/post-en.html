The five main categories of the Spanish Christmas Lottery

 

The Christmas draw hands out five ‘major’ prize categories, numbered from first to fifth, and each one has a fixed amount per décimo and a specific number of prizes. The first prize, which is unique, is not the same as the fifth, of which eight are drawn: that difference is what makes some categories far rarer than others.

The first prize (the Gordo), the second and the third are unique: there is only one winning number in each. From the fourth prize, two numbers are drawn, and from the fifth, eight. That’s why, even though the amounts fall as we move down the categories, the number of lucky winners rises: there are far more décimos holding a fifth prize than the Gordo.

Below the fifth prize come the minor categories — approximations (aproximaciones), hundreds (centenas), endings, the pedrea (small prizes) and the refund prize (reintegro) —, which pay out smaller amounts but to far more numbers. In this guide we focus on the five major categories, the ones that answer the question of ‘how much each prize pays’.

This structure is no accident: the draw is designed to concentrate the biggest prize in a single number — the Gordo, which grabs all the attention — while at the same time spreading substantial amounts among more winners as you move down the categories. It’s the reason the Spanish Christmas Lottery is seen as ‘the one that shares the wealth’: there isn’t a single lucky winner but thousands of winning décimos across the different categories, major and minor.

What are the odds of each prize?

 

Not all prizes are equally easy: the more numbers awarded in a category, the more likely you are to win, and the differences there are large. The odds of each category depend on how many prizes are handed out, always out of the 100,000 numbers in the draw.

If we add up the five major categories, there are thirteen winning numbers in total (one first, one second, one third, two fourths and eight fifths), which gives roughly thirteen chances in 100,000 that your number falls into one of the five big ones. It’s still unlikely, but it’s a useful way to see the whole picture: most of the prizes collected each Christmas are not from these five categories but from the minor ones, which pay out to far more décimos, albeit in more modest amounts.

The first, second and third: 1 in 100,000

The three highest categories — the Gordo, second and third prizes — are unique: there is only one winning number in each. That means the probability that your number is, say, the Gordo is 1 in 100,000 (0.001%). The second and third prizes each carry the same odds on their own. They are the most coveted prizes precisely because they are the hardest: they pay out a lot, but to very few.

The fourth and fifth: considerably more within reach

This is where things change. From the fourth prize, two numbers are drawn, so the odds rise to 2 in 100,000; and from the fifth, eight are drawn, which lifts them to 8 in 100,000 (0.008%). Put another way: you are twice as likely to land a fourth prize as the Gordo, and eight times as likely to land a fifth. They are still hard prizes to win — this is the lottery, after all — but among the major categories they are the most attainable and the ones that bring joy to the most people every 22 December.

How much the fourth and fifth prizes pay (the most sought-after)

 

The fourth prize in the Spanish Christmas Lottery pays €20,000 per décimo, and the fifth pays €6,000 per décimo. These are the two categories we get asked about most, and with good reason: they hand out a very respectable sum and, on top of that, they are the most likely of the five big ones, because there are two fourth prizes and eight fifths.

Think of it in terms of what the décimo cost: for €20, a fourth prize gives you back €20,000 — a thousand times your stake — and a fifth gives you back €6,000, three hundred times. These aren’t life-changing figures like the Gordo, but they are a significant prize that reaches far more people. In fact, at most lottery offices, more fourth and fifth prizes are paid out each year than first prizes, simply because there are more of them.

And there’s a detail many people overlook: because these prizes are spread across more numbers, it’s more common for a single fourth or fifth prize to end up shared among the customers of one lottery office or among a syndicate (peña). That’s why it pays to check your décimo carefully right to the end: a fifth prize that’s ‘small’ compared with the Gordo is still €6,000 you won’t want to leave uncollected.

It’s also worth remembering that the tax charge applies to the higher prizes: only those above €40,000 per décimo are taxed, so neither the fourth (€20,000) nor the fifth (€6,000) pays anything to the Spanish Tax Agency (Hacienda); they are collected in full. That’s another quiet advantage of these mid-range categories: everything you win is yours, with no withholding, and it lands straight in your account or is paid out at the lottery office with no further paperwork.

Beyond the five categories

 

On top of the five major prizes, the draw hands out many other amounts: the approximations to the first, second and third, the hundreds, the last two digits, the pedrea and the refund prize. These are smaller amounts — from €100 per décimo down to the €20 of the refund prize — but they reach far more numbers, to the point that around 15% of décimos win some kind of prize.

We don’t detail them here because this guide focuses on the five main categories, but they’re worth keeping in mind when you check: a décimo may not be ‘one of the five big ones’ and still win a prize through an approximation, the hundreds or the refund prize. Frequently asked questions about the prize categories

 
How much does the fourth prize in the Spanish Christmas Lottery pay?

The fourth prize pays €20,000 per décimo (€200,000 per series). What’s more, there are two fourth prizes in each draw, so the probability of a given number landing a fourth prize is 2 in 100,000. It’s one of the more attainable major categories: it pays out a significant sum and, since there are two, it reaches more people than the top three prizes, which are unique.

How much is the fifth prize in the Spanish Christmas Lottery?

The fifth prize pays €6,000 per décimo (€60,000 per series). It’s the most common major category, because eight fifth prizes are drawn each time: that’s why the odds of landing one (8 in 100,000) are eight times greater than those of the Gordo. It won’t change your life, but for a €20 décimo it’s €6,000, three hundred times your stake.

All three are unique prizes: there is only one winning number in each category, so the probability that your number is one of them is 1 in 100,000 in each case. They are the highest amounts and, at the same time, the hardest to win.

What are the odds of winning each prize?

It depends on how many prizes are handed out, always out of the 100,000 numbers in the draw. The Gordo, the second and the third are unique: 1 in 100,000 each. Is there a number to win the lottery today in 2026?

There isn’t. Every number has the same probability of winning, and there are no ‘lucky’ figures or formulas for getting it right. What is real is understanding how much each category pays and what odds it carries, which is exactly what we explain here, so that you play with clear information. How does the 1220 code work to win?

It doesn’t. The ‘1220 code’ is numerology that circulates on social media and promises to predict the result from previous draws. It’s false: the Christmas draw is pure chance and the décimos are pre-printed, so there is no pattern to ‘calculate’. No method can foresee the Gordo or any other category.
