The Spanish Tax Agency (Hacienda) keeps 20% of the portion of a prize above €40,000, and that percentage is identical for EuroMillions, La Primitiva and the Spanish Christmas Lottery. There is no separate ‘EuroMillions tax’ as opposed to a ‘Christmas Lottery tax’: all three are taxed under the same special tax (gravamen especial). The only thing that changes between them is the size of the prize and, therefore, how much you actually take home.

It’s a perfectly reasonable question, because what’s advertised is always the gross amount: ‘a €100 million jackpot’, ‘€400,000 to the Gordo (top prize) per ticket’. That figure is the prize before tax, not what lands in your account. Knowing how to work out the net amount saves you nasty surprises and, above all, lets you make decisions with the real number in front of you.

In this guide we give you the exact formula, a comparison table with real examples from each game, and answers to the questions we get asked most at the office: what happens with shared prizes, whether you also have to pay personal income tax (IRPF), and who withholds the tax.

You’ll see that, once you understand the mechanism, working out your net prize is one subtraction and one multiplication. Nothing more, nothing less.

We are Official State Lottery Office no. 336 in Barcelona, inside the C.C. Arenas, and every season we’re by the side of anyone who collects a prize. Let’s begin with the essentials: the tax that applies.

The quick answer

€40,000 tax-free, 20% on the rest, and the same tax across all three games

For EuroMillions, La Primitiva and the Christmas Lottery, the first €40,000 of every prize is tax-free and reaches your account in full. Only the part that exceeds that €40,000 is taxed, and it’s taxed at a flat rate of 20%. The tax is withheld at the moment you collect (either the lottery office or the bank does it), so you receive the net amount directly and you don’t have to declare the prize on your tax return.

A single tax for all three games: the special tax

 

EuroMillions, La Primitiva and the Christmas Lottery pay exactly the same tax, because all three are draws run by the Spanish State Lottery (Loterías y Apuestas del Estado, SELAE) and all are subject to the so-called special tax on prizes. There is no higher rate for one game than for another: the idea that ‘EuroMillions pays more’ or ‘the Christmas Lottery has its own special tax’ is a myth.

This special tax is applied automatically and separately from the rest of your income. Its rule is simple and the same for all these games: the first €40,000 of every prize is tax-free and a 20% rate applies to any amount above that limit. It makes no difference whether the prize comes from a EuroMillions slip, a La Primitiva bet or a Christmas Lottery ticket: the calculation is the same.

The same treatment applies to other comparable draws — the Lotería del Niño, El Gordo de la Primitiva, the Bonoloto, and the draws run by the ONCE or Cruz Roja (the Red Cross) — and even to equivalent lotteries organised in other European Union countries. That’s why, when someone asks ‘how much does the Tax Agency keep from EuroMillions?’ and ‘how much does it keep from the Christmas Lottery?’, the underlying answer is the same: 20% of whatever exceeds €40,000.

The formula for working out your net prize

 

Working out what you actually collect is a two-step operation: first you set aside the €40,000 that’s tax-free, then you apply the 20% to the rest. The full formula is: Net = Prize − (Prize − 40,000) × 20%. Here’s the breakdown so you can’t go wrong.

Step 1: set aside the €40,000 that’s tax-free

Of the prize’s gross amount, the first €40,000 is not taxed: it’s entirely yours. If your prize is equal to or below that figure — for example, a fourth prize in the Christmas Lottery of €20,000 per ticket — you pay absolutely nothing and collect the full amount. This tax-free threshold is why most small and medium prizes reach your pocket in one piece.

Step 2: apply the 20% only to what’s left over

A 20% rate applies to the part that exceeds €40,000. That 20% is what the Tax Agency withholds; the remaining 80% of that band is also yours. It’s important to get this right: the 20% is not applied to the whole prize, only to the excess. Getting this wrong is the most common calculation error, and it usually leads people to think they lose far more than they actually hand over.

The €40,000 threshold is per prize, not per person

 

The €40,000 tax-free allowance counts per prize (per ticket, slip or bet), not per person who collects it. This is key with shared prizes — syndicates, fractional shares, tickets split among family — and it’s where most people get their sums wrong.

When a prize is split between several people, the €40,000 tax-free minimum is prorated among them according to their share of participation. In other words, it’s not that each participant enjoys their own €40,000 allowance: the ticket’s €40,000 is divided in proportion to how much each one plays. The practical upshot is that sharing a single ticket does not reduce the total tax on that ticket.

An example makes it clear. Imagine a Gordo ticket of €400,000 split between four friends at 25% each. Each one is due €100,000 gross, with €10,000 tax-free (a quarter of the €40,000). Each pays 20% on €90,000 — that is, €18,000 — and collects €82,000 net. Adding up the four, the total net is €328,000: exactly the same as if a single person had collected the ticket.

✓A shared ticket: a single €40,000 tax-free minimum, prorated among the participants according to their share.

✓Several tickets of the same number: each ticket is a separate prize, with its own €40,000 tax-free.

✗Thinking each participant adds €40,000 tax-free on a shared ticket: that’s not how it works — the allowance is split.

✗One person collecting it and ‘sharing it out’ afterwards with no paperwork: it can look like a gift and be taxed under a different tax.

That’s why, when you play in a group, what matters isn’t just the maths but putting on record who takes part and in what proportion before the draw. That way, if it wins, each person appears as the recipient of their own share and Hacienda applies the proration correctly, without the split being mistaken for a gift between private individuals.

Is there anything else to pay? IRPF, the bank and other questions

 

No, there’s nothing else to pay: the special tax’s 20% is the only tax on the prize, and it isn’t taxed again on your income tax return. The prize isn’t added to your other income, nor does it push up your IRPF rate; the special tax is a separate, final tax that’s settled at the moment you collect.

Because the tax is withheld at source, the process is convenient: when you collect, the lottery office (for smaller prizes) or the bank (for larger amounts) already deducts the corresponding 20% and pays you the net. You don’t have to complete any extra paperwork or advance money to Hacienda yourself.

→ Not declared on your tax return: the prize has already been taxed under the special tax and isn’t included in your IRPF.

→ Automatic withholding: the 20% on the excess is deducted when you collect; you receive the net amount directly.

→ Mind what you do afterwards: if you invest the money, any returns it generates (interest, dividends, rent) are indeed taxed like any other savings.

→ Document shared prizes: identifying the co-owners before collecting stops a split from being read as a gift.

In short: on the prize itself, a single tax — 20% of whatever exceeds €40,000 — and nothing more. The complications you sometimes hear about usually come from what’s done with the money afterwards, not from the prize.

Frequently asked questions about how prizes are taxed

 
How much does the Tax Agency keep from EuroMillions?

The same as any other SELAE draw: the first €40,000 is tax-free and the rest is taxed at 20%. For example, on a €100,000,000 jackpot, the tax withheld would be 20% of €99,960,000 — that is, €19,992,000 — and you’d collect €80,008,000 net. There’s no higher special tax just because it’s EuroMillions: the percentage is identical to La Primitiva or the Christmas Lottery; what changes is the size of the prize.

How much does the Tax Agency keep from the Christmas Lottery?

Exactly the same tax: €40,000 tax-free and 20% on the excess for each winning ticket. On the Gordo, which pays out €400,000 per ticket, €72,000 is withheld (20% of €360,000) and you collect €328,000 net. Prizes that don’t reach €40,000 per ticket — like the fourth prize, at €20,000 — are fully tax-free and collected in full.

Do you have to pay IRPF on top of the 20% on a lottery prize?

No. The 20% special tax is the only tax on the prize, and it’s settled at the moment you collect through withholding at source. The prize is not included on your income tax return, nor does it raise your IRPF rate. What you do afterwards with the money is a different matter: if you invest it, any returns it generates (interest, dividends, rent) will be taxed like any other savings, but that’s no longer the tax on the prize.

How is a shared lottery prize (syndicate or share) taxed?

The €40,000 tax-free minimum is prorated among the participants according to their percentage. On a Gordo ticket of €400,000 split between four people at 25% each, each one receives €100,000 gross, with €10,000 tax-free, pays 20% on €90,000 (€18,000) and collects €82,000 net. Sharing a single ticket doesn’t multiply the allowance. The essential thing is to document who takes part and in what proportion before the draw, so each person collects their share and it isn’t read as a gift.

Is there a number to win the lottery today in 2026?

There isn’t, and anyone who promises one is deceiving you. Every number has the same probability: there are no ‘lucky’ figures and no combinations that guarantee a prize. What is real and verifiable — and the reason for this guide — is how much you’d collect net if you won: that can be worked out precisely. Put your energy into understanding your real prize, not into hunting for a magic number that doesn’t exist.

How does the 1220 code work to win?

It doesn’t work: the ‘1220 code’ is numerology that circulates on social media and promises to predict results from past draws. It’s false, because each draw is pure chance and the tickets are pre-printed; there is no pattern to ‘calculate’. No method can foresee the Gordo or the EuroMillions jackpot. The only thing genuinely worth calculating is your net after the tax, and for that a subtraction and a 20% are enough.
