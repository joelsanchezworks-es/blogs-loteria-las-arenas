A homemade lucky charm doesn’t change your objective luck, but it can change how you feel: it gives you calm, confidence and optimism, and that, at work, has a very real effect. The key is to tell two worlds apart: the world of things that depend on you —a meeting, an interview, a sale—, where your attitude matters and a charm can help you as a knock-on effect; and the world of pure chance —a weekly draw—, where no stone, clover or ritual moves the needle a single millimetre.

We like to think we’re very rational, but almost all of us have our little ritual: the pen kept for the important signature, the shirt ‘that brings luck’, the coin in the wallet. It isn’t nonsense, nor plain superstition: it’s the brain doing what it does best, looking for a sense of control when the outcome isn’t entirely in our hands.

In this guide we’ll tell you, with a dash of humour and a dash of science, what the research says about lucky charms, which ones are most common for luck at work, and why they work (when they work). The quick answer

A charm is a placebo that improves you, not the odds

A homemade charm works like a placebo: it doesn’t change reality, but it changes your state of mind. By giving you confidence and calm, it can help you with tasks that depend on you —an interview, an exam, a negotiation—, because a better attitude really does improve the result. What a charm cannot do is alter the pure chance of a weekly draw: in EuroMillions, La Primitiva or La Bonoloto, the odds are the same with a charm or without one. In short: use it for the calm it gives you, not for the results it promises.

Do lucky charms work? What science says

 

The short answer is: they can help you, but not by magic —rather by what they do inside your head. Psychology has studied this phenomenon, and what it finds is as fascinating as it is down to earth.

The placebo effect and self-efficacy

The most cited study on this (Damisch and colleagues, 2010) tested a number of people on tasks of dexterity, memory and word games, letting some keep their lucky charm and others not. Those who had it performed a little better, and the explanation wasn’t magical: they felt more capable. It’s what psychology calls self-efficacy, the confidence in your own ability to pull something off. The charm didn’t touch the task; it touched the person, who tried harder, kept at it longer and got less nervous. At work, where so much depends on attitude, that boost of confidence is perfectly real.

The honest caveat: the science isn’t conclusive

Now for the part no charm seller will tell you: that effect is fragile and hard to replicate. Later studies tried to repeat those results and, in several cases, the advantage vanished: people who believed they had a lucky object performed practically the same as everyone else. So let’s be honest: the charm isn’t a reliable performance tool, and it’s certainly not magic. What does seem to hold up is the most modest and the most useful thing at once —that it reduces anxiety and raises optimism—, and that, on a rough day at the office, is no small thing.

Homemade lucky charms for luck at work

 

You don’t need to buy anything expensive: the charms that work best are the homemade ones, because their value lies in what they mean to you, not in the object. Here are some of the most common ones for the professional world, and they all share the same ‘mechanism’: being a small anchor of confidence before an important moment.

→ A stone or smooth pebble in your pocket: smooth and pleasant to touch, it works to shed nerves before speaking in public.

→ The ‘lucky’ pen: the one you save for signing contracts or for exams, and that puts you in ‘this is serious’ mode.

→ A coin in your wallet: a tradition of ‘attracting money’ that, above all, reminds you of your goals every time you see it.

→ A plant on your desk: from the pothos to ‘lucky bamboo’; beyond the myth, caring for something green really does cut stress.

→ A personal object with a story: a photo, a keyring, a small gift; its ‘power’ is to remind you who you are and why you do it.

Notice that none of these charms ‘does’ anything on its own: they all work through you, through your mood and your focus. That’s why the best charm isn’t the prettiest or the most expensive, but the one that brings you calm. If a smooth pebble in your pocket helps you walk into a meeting more relaxed, it has done its job, and there’s nothing wrong with that.

Why the brain seeks control (even when there is none)

 

We reach for charms and rituals because the human brain hates uncertainty and needs to feel it has some control, especially when money or work is on the line. It’s an ancient and very useful mechanism: manufacturing a sense of control reduces anxiety and lets us keep going under pressure, even when that control is partly imaginary.

The trouble starts when we mix up the two situations we talked about at the beginning. Facing an interview, feeling that you ‘control’ something helps you prepare and rise to the occasion. Facing a draw, that same feeling is an illusion: you can have every ritual in the world, but the ball doesn’t know you exist. Recognising the difference doesn’t take the fun out of charms; it simply stops you from making decisions (or spending money) in the belief that they tip a scale that, in reality, never moves.

The professional and financial arena is especially fertile ground for this, because it blends things you do control with things you don’t, all mixed together. Your daily work depends on your effort; promotions, on other people’s decisions; and an investment or a prize, in good part, on factors completely beyond you. In that cocktail of control and chance, the mind tends to stretch the ritual that works in the first case (‘my lucky pen for signing’) to the second (‘my lucky number for the draw’), without noticing that only in one of them is there something real behind it. Telling where your influence ends and chance begins is, probably, the most practical piece of advice in this whole article.

Frequently asked questions about lucky charms and luck

 
Do homemade lucky charms work for luck at work?

They can help you, but not by magic. A charm acts as a placebo: by giving you confidence and calm, it improves your attitude, and at work —an interview, a meeting, a sale— attitude really does bear on the result. Psychology links it to self-efficacy, the belief in your own ability. That said, the effect is modest and hard to measure: the charm doesn’t make you more competent, it can only help you give your best with fewer nerves.

What homemade lucky charms can I have at work?

The most common and cheapest: a smooth stone or pebble in your pocket to shed nerves, a ‘lucky’ pen for important moments, a coin in your wallet, a plant on your desk (caring for something green really does cut stress) or a personal object with a story. None of them has powers: they work as small anchors of confidence. The best one isn’t the prettiest, but the one that brings you calm.

Does a charm raise my chances of winning the lottery?

No, not at all. A draw is pure chance and the odds on your ticket are fixed: the same with a charm as without one. The draw drum has no idea what you’re carrying in your pocket. Charms can influence things that depend on your attitude, but a draw doesn’t depend on you in any way, so there their effect is exactly zero. You can carry your charm for the excitement, but not as a strategy to win.

Why do we like having lucky charms so much?

Because the human brain hates uncertainty and looks to feel it has control, especially when money or work is at stake. Manufacturing a sense of control —even a partly illusory one— reduces anxiety and helps us function under pressure. It’s a normal mechanism and, properly understood, even a useful one: the problem only turns up if we confuse the situations we can influence with the ones that are pure chance, like a draw.

Is there a number to win the lottery today in 2026?

There isn’t, neither with a charm nor without one. Every number has the same probability, and there are no ‘lucky’ figures, objects or formulas that guarantee a prize. If a number or a charm gives you a good feeling, that’s great for the excitement, but it doesn’t change the odds. Anyone who sells you ‘today’s winning number’ is fooling you; the winner is only known once it comes out of the draw drum.

How does the 1220 code work to win?

It doesn’t work. The ‘1220 code’ is numerology that goes around on social media and promises to predict results from previous draws. It’s as magical as a four-leaf clover: that is, not at all. Every draw is pure chance and the bets are independent, so there’s no pattern to ‘calculate’. No method and no charm can guess the result. The only useful thing is to play with full guarantees and check your bets properly.
