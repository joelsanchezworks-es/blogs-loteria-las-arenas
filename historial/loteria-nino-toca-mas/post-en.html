Yes, El Niño really does ‘pay out’ more often than the Christmas Lottery (Lotería de Navidad): nearly 4 in 10 tickets (décimos) win some prize, against roughly 15% at Christmas. But the reason is not luck — it is a quirk of the rules, because El Niño (the 6 January Epiphany / Three Kings’ Day draw) hands out three times as many refunds. And there is the trap in the myth: collecting a refund (reintegro — the prize that just returns your stake) is not winning, it is simply getting back what you put in.

The Reyes special draw — El Niño — is held every 6 January and carries a reputation for being ‘generous’. That reputation is deserved in one very specific sense: statistically, it is the draw in which the most tickets come away with something in hand. But ‘something in hand’ and ‘winning money’ are not the same thing, and that difference is exactly the one almost nobody explains to you.

In this guide we do the counter maths: we put El Niño and Christmas head to head — refunds, odds and prizes — and explain why El Niño is, for the average player, the draw with the best ‘return’… and why that still does not make it an investment. The quick answer

El Niño pays out more because it has three refunds, not because it brings more luck

At Christmas there is one refund (the last digit of El Gordo, the Christmas top prize): you get your €20 back with a probability of 1 in 10. El Niño has three refunds, so that probability rises to about 30%. That is why it pays out on far more tickets. But a refund only returns your stake: it is not a prize, it is breaking even. El Niño is the draw with the best odds of not losing; Christmas, the one with the biggest prizes.

The figure that feeds the myth

 

The probability that an El Niño ticket wins some prize is around 38% if we count refunds; at Christmas, that figure stays close to 15%. In round numbers: with El Niño, nearly 4 in 10 tickets ‘come up a winner’; at Christmas, barely 1 in 7. This is not a shop-counter legend — it is the structure of the draw.

The nuance matters a great deal. If we strip out the refunds and count only the ‘real’ prizes, El Niño’s probability drops to around 7.8%. In other words, most of that 38% is refunds — tickets that recover the €20 and little more. The draw spreads joy across many hands, but almost always in the form of ‘here, that’s what you put in’.

And it is not all refunds: El Niño also hands out an enormous number of small prizes. Between the first prize (€200,000 per ticket), the second (€75,000), the third (€25,000), the approximations (aproximaciones) to the top prizes — €1,200 and €610 — and the three- and four-digit extractions (extracciones — last-digit prize tiers), at €100 and €350, the 2026 draw paid out around €700 million across more than 36,000 prizes. It is a fine drizzle: many modest prizes rather than a few enormous ones.

Even so, the psychological effect is real: coming away empty-handed stings, and El Niño leaves far fewer people empty-handed. That is why it has a reputation for being ‘rewarding’. The counter question is whether that makes it better than Christmas, and the answer — as with almost everything in the lottery — is ‘it depends what you are after’.

The key is in the refunds

 

El Niño’s entire statistical ‘superpower’ fits in one word: refunds. Where Christmas has one, El Niño has three, and that triples the odds of your ticket recovering its stake. It is worth understanding exactly what that very humble prize is, because it is the one that tips the balance. And it is no accident: the draw is designed precisely to leave fewer people empty-handed and keep the festive excitement alive right up to Three Kings’ Day itself.

What a refund is (and why it is not winning)

A refund is the prize that matches the last digit of a winning number and returns exactly what you paid for the ticket: €20. Not a euro more. If you stake €20 and the refund comes up, you go home with your €20: you have ‘broken even’, not won. So when someone says ‘El Niño came up for me’, it is worth asking ‘how much?’: very often the answer is, quite simply, what they had put in. Even so, the refund counts as a prize for statistical purposes, and that is where those sky-high ‘winning ticket’ percentages in the January headlines come from.

Three numbers that change everything

In El Niño, three refund digits are drawn instead of one. Since each digit covers 10% of the tickets (those ending in that number), three different digits cover around 30%. In 2026, for example, the winning refunds were 3, 1 and 0: any ticket ending in 3, 1 or 0 recovered its stake. That simple ‘x3’ is what makes El Niño the draw where you most often hear ‘something is something’. It is, at heart, the cheapest way the draw has of spreading hope: instead of a single refund as at Christmas, it triples the digits and, with them, the number of people who at least do not lose.

Careful not to confuse ‘a payout’ with ‘a win’. Getting your €20 refund back is fine — it means not losing — but it is not a prize in the sense of winning money. When you compare draws, look at two separate things: the probability that ‘something lands’ and the size of that something. They do not always go together.

So which one really pays out more?

 

It depends what you mean by ‘pay out’. If it means ‘getting anything at all, even just my stake back’, El Niño wins hands down. If it means ‘winning real money’, the two draws are equally hard, and Christmas hands out the biggest prizes. There is no trick: there are two ways of measuring the same thing.

And here comes the part that does not change however much the probability rises: both draws have a negative expected value (a negative mathematical expectation). As in any lottery, less than a euro is paid out in prizes for every euro staked; the rest goes on taxes and the share the State keeps. El Niño gives you better odds of recovering something, but it does not overturn that rule. In the long run, you play to take part, not to win.

There is another way to see it, in money. El Niño pays out less in total than Christmas — around €700 million, against more than three times that at Christmas — but it spreads it across far more tickets. Christmas concentrates; El Niño scatters. That is why at Christmas it is harder for your number to come up, but when the Gordo lands it changes a whole life; and why with El Niño there is a payout often, even if it is almost always just to give you your stake back.

Put another way: El Niño is the draw with the best return for the average player — more tickets go home with something — but ‘best return’ does not mean ‘positive return’. It means you lose less often, not that you win. It is a small difference in words and an enormous one in your wallet.

The counter maths: which draw suits you

 

There is no ‘best’ draw; there is one that fits what you are after better. After many Januaries behind the counter, this is what we tend to say:

✓El Niño is for you if you prefer better odds that ‘something lands’ and are happy with a more widely shared draw and lower highs.

✓El Niño is for you if you play for the thrill of taking part in the last big draw of the festive season and all but avoiding an empty-handed finish.

✗El Niño is NOT your draw if what keeps you awake is the big windfall: there Christmas, with its Gordo, plays in another league.

✓Christmas is for you if you buy the ticket to dream of changing your life, accepting that the likeliest outcome is getting nothing back.

✗Neither is for you as an investment: both have a negative expected value. Play with a leisure budget, never expecting a profit.

In short: El Niño is the draw that is kindest to your pocket in the short term, and Christmas the one for dreaming big. Whichever you choose, the counter advice is the same: play only what you have budgeted to enjoy, live the draw in good company, and treat any prize — refund included — as what it is, an unexpected extra, and never as a savings plan.

Frequently asked questions about El Niño lottery

 
Why does El Niño lottery pay out more often than the Christmas one?

Because it has three refunds instead of one. A refund rewards the tickets whose last digit matches a drawn number, and each digit covers 10% of the tickets. With three winning digits, about 30% of tickets recover their stake, against the 10% at Christmas. Counting every prize, El Niño pays out on close to 38% of tickets, against roughly 15% at Christmas. That said: most of those prizes are refunds — that is, recovering the €20 staked.

How many refunds does El Niño lottery have?

Three. Three refund digits are drawn (in 2026 they were 3, 1 and 0), and any ticket whose last digit matches one of them recovers the €20 staked. Since each digit covers 10% of the numbers, three different digits add up to around a 30% chance of a refund. The Christmas Lottery, by contrast, has only one refund: the last digit of the Gordo.

Is it better to play El Niño or the Christmas Lottery?

It depends what you are after. El Niño gives better odds that ‘something lands’ (above all refunds) and shares out more widely; it is the draw with the best chance of not leaving empty-handed. Christmas hands out the biggest individual prizes — the Gordo is €400,000 per ticket against the €200,000 of El Niño’s first prize — but with less chance of your number coming up. Neither is an investment: both have a negative expected value, so the sensible thing is to play for fun and with a leisure budget.

Does collecting the refund count as winning the lottery?

Technically you do receive a prize, but the refund only gives back what you paid for the ticket (€20), so you win no money: you ‘break even’. That is why it is worth not confusing ‘it came up for me’ with ‘I won’. When you compare the ‘generosity’ of two draws, look separately at the probability that something lands and the size of that something: El Niño wins on the first, but a good part of its prizes are precisely refunds.

Is there a number to win the lottery today, in 2026?

There is none — neither a number nor a method. Every draw, El Niño included, is pure chance: there is no figure, system or ‘code’ that guarantees a prize, and anyone who had one would not sell it. El Niño ‘paying out more’ is down to no trick, but to the fact that it hands out more refunds; it is still impossible to know which number will come up. The only sensible thing is to play for fun, with a clear head and a leisure budget.

How does the 1220 code to win work?

It does not work. The ‘1220 code’ is numerology doing the rounds on social media, promising to predict results, and it is just one more of the false foolproof methods. Every draw is independent and random, El Niño among them, so there is no pattern to calculate and no code to guess it. El Niño’s only real ‘mathematical’ advantage is its three refunds, and that is in the rulebook, in plain sight, not in any secret code.
