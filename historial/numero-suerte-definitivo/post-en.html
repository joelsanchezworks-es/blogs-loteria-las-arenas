Is there an ultimate lucky number?

 

No. In a fair draw, each number has the same probability as any other, and that probability does not change no matter how much we like a number, how much history it carries or how ‘lucky’ it feels. This is the piece of real mathematics worth getting straight before anything else.

The reason is that each draw is independent and the figures are equally likely. In the Spanish Christmas Lottery the numbers 00000 to 99999 are issued, and the draw drum picks one without knowing whether it is pretty, ugly, round or a match for your birthday. The probability that yours comes up is one in 100,000, no more and no less, whatever it happens to be called. There is no hidden ‘lucky’ property tucked inside the digits: a number is only a label.

So when someone asks ‘what is the lucky number?’, the honest answer is that the best number is the one you feel like playing. Not because it is more likely —it is not— but because the thrill is free, and playing something that makes you smile costs no more than playing anything else.

The most-searched numbers in Spain (and why)

 

Even though they are all worth the same, they do not all sell the same: every year some numbers fly off the shelves and some endings sell out, and the reasons are always emotional. Here are the big groups of ‘most-searched numbers’.

Special dates: birthdays, weddings and 25.12

The most searched of all are meaningful dates: a birthday, a wedding anniversary, the birth of a child. Since the days of the month run from 1 to 31 and the months from 1 to 12, numbers that begin with 0 or 1 sell out far sooner than the rest, and combinations like 25.12 (Christmas) or 01.01 (New Year) vanish almost at once. It is not that they are more likely: it is that a huge number of people are hunting for their own date, and those particular numbers are limited.

Palindromes, ‘pretty’ numbers and topical ones

Then come the ones sought for their aesthetics: palindromes (which read the same forwards and backwards, like 12321 or 45654), the very repetitive ones (11111), round numbers, and ‘staircases’ (12345). They catch the eye and sell out for the sheer visual pleasure of them. And every year the topical numbers appear: the shirt number of a champion in the spotlight, the figure tied to a much-talked-about news story or to an event of the year. These are passing phenomena —they change from one Christmas to the next— and they carry no advantage: they simply mirror whatever is filling the conversation at that moment.

‘Hot’ endings and ‘cursed’ numbers: the great misunderstanding

 

Here is the most repeated blunder of all: believing that an ending which has come up a lot is more likely, or that a number that ‘never wins’ is jinxed. Neither is true. It is true that there are curious facts —for example, the ending in 5 is the one that has come up most often in the Christmas Gordo (top prize), some 32 times, followed by 4 and 6— but that fact belongs to the past and says absolutely nothing about the next draw.

Chance has no memory. The fact that 5 has come up a lot does not make it ‘hotter’ for next year, just as a coin that has landed heads ten times is no more likely to come up tails (or heads) on the next toss. Every draw starts from zero. Confusing ‘what has happened’ with ‘what is going to happen’ is what psychology calls the gambler’s fallacy, and it is the mental trap that drives most of the ‘systems’ and ‘hot numbers’ sold out there.

With ‘cursed’ numbers the same thing happens in reverse. 13 drags along a reputation for being jinxed, and yet the ending 13 is among the first to sell out every Christmas, because a lot of people get a kick out of playing precisely on the thrill of the forbidden. Reality is stubborn: 13 is neither cursed nor blessed. It has the same probability as 7, as 22 or as any other.

No ending is ‘hot’ and no number is ‘cursed’. Past data is a curiosity, not a prediction: each draw is independent and every number keeps the same probability, one in 100,000. Choosing by ‘the ones that come up most’ does not improve your odds one little bit.

Birth-date numerology: science or entertainment?

 

Numerology —reducing your date of birth to a single digit to find your ‘personal number’— is a widespread cultural pastime, but it has no power whatsoever to predict a draw. It works like this: you add up all the digits of the date and keep reducing until you get a number from 1 to 9. For example, someone born on 25/12/1990 would add 2+5+1+2+1+9+9+0 = 29, then 2+9 = 11, and 1+1 = 2; their ‘number’ would be 2.

It is a charming game and many people find it comforting, just like a horoscope. But it is wise not to confuse entertainment with strategy: there is no relationship between your numerology number and the result of the draw drum. If you enjoy using it to pick your décimo, go ahead —it does no harm and the thrill is free— the one thing you should not expect is for it to improve your odds, because it does not. On that score, numerology sits at exactly the same level as closing your eyes and pointing at a number at random.

Frequently asked questions about the lucky number

 
What is the lucky number?
