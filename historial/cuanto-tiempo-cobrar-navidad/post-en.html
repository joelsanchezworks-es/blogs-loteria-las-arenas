The quick answer

Three months from the day after the draw, and not a day more

The deadline to claim any Spanish Christmas Lottery prize — from the €20 refund prize (reintegro) to the Gordo (top prize) — is three months (90 days), counted from the day after the draw. For a draw held on 22 December, the deadline falls around 23 March. It is a non-extendable deadline: it allows no exceptions for illness, forgetfulness or any other circumstance. If you don’t collect in time, the prize expires and the money reverts to the Public Treasury.

Exactly how long do you have to claim?

 

The right to claim a Spanish Christmas Lottery prize expires after three months, counted from the day after the draw is held. This is the rule set by the Spanish State Lottery (Loterías y Apuestas del Estado), and it applies equally to every prize, regardless of the amount: it makes no difference whether you have won the reintegro or the first prize — the deadline is the same.

It’s worth paying attention to that phrase, ‘the day after’. If the draw is held on 22 December, the count begins on 23 December and the three months end around 23 March the following year. That is your real deadline: the last day a lottery office or a bank will pay out your prize. After that, no institution can pay it.

Three months is more than enough to collect at your leisure, and in fact there’s no need to rush on the 22nd itself: it’s better to check and claim without hurrying, with the official list in front of you. But that generous window is, paradoxically, exactly what makes people relax and put it off ‘until later’… until they forget. The deadline doesn’t forgive slip-ups.

From which day are the three months counted?

The count doesn’t begin on the day of the draw, but the day after. For the draw on 22 December, the clock starts on 23 December and stops three months later, around 23 March. Working out your exact date is easy: count three months from the day after the draw, and that is your last day to collect. Our practical advice is that, as soon as you confirm a décimo has won, note that deadline in your calendar or on your phone and, if the prize is large or shared, don’t leave it until the end. A thirty-second reminder saves you from the one mistake in this whole process that has no remedy.

What happens to prizes that go unclaimed?

 

Prizes that aren’t collected within the deadline expire, and their value automatically reverts to the Public Treasury — in other words, it goes back into the State’s coffers. That money is folded into public funds and spent on the same things as the rest of the State’s income: healthcare, education, infrastructure and other services. Put another way: an unclaimed prize is not ‘set aside’ for you, nor shared out among other players; it simply stops being yours.

And these are not isolated cases. According to the Spanish State Lottery’s own figures, between €60 and €80 million goes uncollected every year in lottery prizes across Spain, much of it from the National Lottery and its draws, such as the Christmas one. It’s not all people who failed to check: there are winning tickets that were lost, damaged, or whose owner never realised they had won.

The most important thing to take away is this: there is no mechanism to collect an expired prize. Even if you keep the décimo and it’s proven to be valid, once the deadline has passed there is no claim, appeal or exception that counts. That’s why the only real protection is not letting that day arrive without having collected.

The risk of a shared décimo without safekeeping

 

The biggest expiry risk isn’t with the tickets you play on your own, but with the shared ones: shares in a syndicate, décimos among family or friends, where often no one is clear on who is keeping the slip. This is where prizes that had genuinely been won are lost, year after year.

The pattern repeats itself: someone buys a décimo and snaps a photo for the WhatsApp group, shares are handed out ‘on a handshake’, and when the moment of truth arrives, the problems appear. Who has the physical décimo? Does everyone’s idea of what they chipped in match up? And what if that person has moved, is away travelling or, in the worst case, has passed away? A photo on your phone is not enough to collect: the prize is paid to whoever presents the décimo, and without it — or without a well-documented split — the money can stay frozen until it expires.

In a syndicate of twenty people, the problem isn’t only losing the décimo: it’s that a single disagreement or absence is enough to tangle up the payout just as the three-month clock is already ticking. The solution isn’t to play less as a group — sharing the lottery is one of the finest Christmas traditions — but to do it with your back covered.

Why a photo of the décimo isn’t enough to collect

Many people think that sending a photo of the décimo to the group covers everything, and it doesn’t. The prize is paid against the physical décimo (or against registered official safekeeping), because the décimo itself is the document that proves the right to collect; an image is not valid to present for payment. This has two practical consequences: if the slip is lost or damaged, collecting becomes enormously complicated; and if a single person keeps it ‘for the group’, everyone else depends on them turning up in time. The photo is good for knowing which number you are playing, but not for collecting it: for that you need the décimo or a safekeeping arrangement that puts on record who is taking part.

Frequently asked questions about the deadline to claim the Christmas Lottery

 
How long do you have to claim the Spanish Christmas Lottery?

The deadline is three months (90 days), counted from the day after the draw. For a draw held on 22 December, the deadline falls around 23 March the following year. It applies equally to every prize, from the €20 reintegro to the Gordo, and it is non-extendable: there are no exceptions for illness, travel or forgetfulness. Once that date passes, no lottery office or bank can pay the prize.

When do Spanish Christmas Lottery prizes expire?

They expire three months after the day following the draw. That is, if the draw is on 22 December, prizes can be collected until roughly 23 March; from that day on, the right to collect lapses. The exact date varies each year with the calendar, but the rule is always the same. As soon as you confirm a décimo has won, it is wise to note its expiry date so you don’t let it slip by.

What happens to lottery prizes that aren’t collected?

The value of an unclaimed prize reverts to the Public Treasury — that is, it goes back into the State’s coffers and is spent on public services. It isn’t shared out among other players, nor kept for the holder. According to figures from the Spanish State Lottery, between €60 and €80 million goes uncollected every year in prizes across Spain. And there is no mechanism to recover an expired prize, even if you keep the décimo and it’s proven to have been valid.

What happens if I lose a winning décimo or don’t know who’s keeping it?

The prize is collected by whoever presents the physical décimo (or holds it in official safekeeping registered in their name). A WhatsApp photo doesn’t let you collect. That’s why décimos shared ‘on a handshake’ carry the most risk: if the slip is lost or the person keeping it can’t be reached, the payout can be frozen until the prize expires. The best protection is to safeguard the décimo and record the ownership of each share before the draw.

Is there a number to win the lottery today in 2026?

There isn’t. Every number has the same probability, and there are no ‘lucky’ figures or formulas for getting it right. What does depend on you, and is very real, is not losing a prize you’ve already won: checking carefully, safeguarding the décimo and collecting within the deadline. That’s where money is truly won or lost — not in some magic number someone wants to sell you.

How does the 1220 code work to win?

It doesn’t. The ‘1220 code’ is numerology that circulates on social media and claims to predict results from past draws. It’s false: every draw is pure chance and the décimos are pre-printed, so there is no pattern to ‘work out’. No method predicts the Gordo. The only genuinely useful thing is to look after the décimo you already have and collect it in time, before the three months are up.
