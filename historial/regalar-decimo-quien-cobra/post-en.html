That is, the prize belongs to whoever presents the physical décimo, not necessarily to whoever paid for it. That's why, when you give it, the most important thing is to make clear whose it is.

Who collects a gifted décimo if it wins?

 

The lottery ticket is a bearer instrument (al portador): whoever presents the décimo collects. If you give it to someone and that person has it, they're the one who can collect it. Legally it doesn't 'go with you' because you paid for it: it goes with the décimo.

This has a good side —giving a gift is very easy— and a delicate side: if it isn't clear that it was a gift, or if several people feel they own the same décimo, there can be misunderstandings at the worst possible moment. That's why it's best to talk it through from the start.

What to make clear when you gift a décimo

 

Gifting a décimo well is, above all, about making two things clear: that it's a gift and whose it becomes. With that, you avoid almost any problem.

→ That it's an outright gift: if you give it to someone, the décimo (and any prize it may win) is theirs. Say so naturally when you hand it over.

→ Who the owner is: if you share it among several people, put in writing who's in and with what share, before the draw.

→ Keep proof: a photo of the décimo, a message or a note with the date help prove whose it was if needed.

How to avoid disputes (and trouble with Hacienda)

 

The tax problem usually isn't the gift itself, but splitting a prize that, on paper, belonged to a single person. If one person collects and then 'gives' a share to others who weren't listed as owners, Hacienda could treat it as a gift.

To avoid this, the key is to make ownership clear before the draw: if the décimo is shared, record who takes part and with what percentage. That way, when a big prize is collected, the bank can distribute it directly among the registered owners. If you have doubts about a significant prize, consult a professional: we also explain it, in more detail, in sharing lottery on WhatsApp: the legal risks.

And if the décimo was shared?

If several people play the same décimo, the safest thing is to document it before the draw: a list of the participants and their shares, or collecting it through a bank that identifies all the owners. That way each person receives their share directly and the distribution isn't interpreted as a gift. Remember: this is general guidance; for large amounts, it's better to get advice.

Frequently asked questions about gifting a décimo and who collects the prize

 
If I gift a décimo and it wins, who collects the prize?

In principle, whoever has the décimo: the lottery ticket is a bearer instrument (al portador), so the prize belongs to whoever presents the physical décimo. If you gave it as a gift, it belongs to the person you gave it to. That's why it's best to make clear that it's a gift when you hand it over.

Do you have to pay tax for gifting a décimo?

Gifting a décimo (€20) usually isn't much of a problem. The question arises when splitting a prize: if one person collects and then gives a share to others who weren't recorded as owners, it may be considered a gift. The rules vary by autonomous region; for large amounts, consult a professional.

How do I prove a décimo was a gift?

With any simple proof: a photo of the décimo, a message or a note with the date you handed it over. Nothing formal is needed for an ordinary gift, but having a record helps if any doubt comes up.

And if the décimo was shared among several people?

The safest thing is to put it in writing before the draw: who takes part and with what share. If a big prize wins, it can be collected through the bank, which distributes it among the registered owners. That way the distribution isn't interpreted as a gift.

Is it a good idea to send a photo of the décimo by WhatsApp?

As a keepsake, yes; as 'proof' of ownership, it has its risks. A photo doesn't establish who the owner is and can cause confusion. We look at this in our article on sharing lottery on WhatsApp and its legal risks.
