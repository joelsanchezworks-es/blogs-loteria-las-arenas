The quick answer

A fallen ball doesn't change the draw: the physical ball rules, not the voice

If a ball falls out or jams, the chanting stops, the ball is retrieved without ever leaving anyone's sight, it is shown to the head table and the cameras to prove it is the same one, and it is put back in by hand. If a child chants the wrong number, nothing happens either: what counts is not what you hear, but the ball that has physically come out, verified by the head table and the notary. Exactly what happens when a ball falls out of the drum

 

There is a single golden rule: the ball cannot disappear from anyone's sight at any moment. The entire protocol revolves around that idea, because what is being protected is not the ball itself, but the certainty that no one could have swapped it. These are the steps:

1

The chanting stops

The moment the ball touches the floor or jams, the rhythm of the draw halts. Nobody carries on chanting as if nothing had happened: the whole routine freezes until the incident is resolved.

2

It is retrieved without being lost from sight

The child, often assisted by a key-holder or by lottery staff, locates and picks up the ball straight away. At no point does it leave the head table's field of vision.

3

It is shown to the head table and the cameras

Before carrying on, the ball is shown to the head table and to the television cameras. That is the gesture that certifies, before the notary and the public, that it is exactly the same ball and that there has been no switch.

4

It is put back in by hand

Once everything has been checked, the operator in charge drops the ball back into the drum and the draw resumes. Chance is left intact.

The ball that jammed in 2020 and the one that fell in 2022

 

This isn't theory: it has happened, and live on air. In the 2020 draw, a ball got stuck and delayed the start; it was the official auditor herself who saw to placing it correctly in the drum before things began. The draw started a few minutes late, and that was all.

In 2022, a ball jumped out of the drum during an extraction and social media caught fire, as it does every time something like this happens. But the outcome was the usual one: it was picked up, it was verified and the draw carried on without the result being affected. What is a tense moment for the viewer is, for the technical staff, a rehearsed procedure resolved in seconds.

The lesson of both cases is the same: the system is designed so that a mechanical incident is an anecdote, not a problem. Precisely because everything is so closely watched, it would be strange if a scare like this changed anything.

It is worth remembering that the draw lasts for hours and strings together thousands of extractions over the course of the morning. The fact that in more than two centuries of history the incidents can almost be counted on one hand, and that every one has been resolved without affecting the result, says a great deal about how well oiled the machinery is. What social media lives as 'chaos in the drum' is, in reality, a rarity that has been perfectly anticipated.

And if a San Ildefonso schoolchild chants a number wrong?

 

Here is the public's great misunderstanding: the children's voices are symbolic; they are not what decides the prize. What determines the result is the ball that physically comes out of the drum, not what is chanted. If there is a mistake while chanting, it is spotted and corrected, but the draw is not repeated.

The voice is the tradition; the ball is the law

Each ball has its number or its prize engraved on it, and its extraction is supervised at all times by the head table and the notary. If a child chants the wrong figure, mixes up a prize or announces something that doesn't match, the error is spotted because the balls are being verified in parallel. The routine pauses for a moment, the extracted ball is checked and the matter is cleared up before carrying on. What is chanted may stir the emotions, but it doesn't pay out: what pays is what the ball says.

What the cameras don't tell you

 

The great 'trick' of the Christmas Lottery draw is that there is no trick, and it takes a great deal of invisible work to guarantee that. Before it begins, the balls are inspected so that they all weigh and measure exactly the same; if one is damaged, it is replaced. The drums are kept for the rest of the year at the Royal Mint (Fábrica Nacional de Moneda y Timbre). And during the draw, every extraction passes before the eyes of the head table, the notary and millions of viewers.

There is another detail that explains that apparent composure: the technical staff know the protocol inside out. Picking up a ball, showing it to the head table, putting it back… none of this is improvised on the day of the draw; it is a procedure that is planned and known by heart. That is why, when it happens live, it is resolved with a matter-of-factness that strikes the startled viewer as almost unbelievable.

So when a ball drops and social media fills with theories, the staff inside barely bat an eyelid: for them it is procedure number such-and-such, foreseen in advance. The transparency of the draw rests not on nothing ever failing mechanically, but on the fact that, when something does fail, there is a public, verifiable protocol that resolves it in full view of everyone. That is the exact opposite of a secret.
