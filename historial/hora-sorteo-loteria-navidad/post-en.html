What time does the Spanish Christmas Lottery draw start?

From then on, the draw doesn't stop until the very end: the prizes are sung out one after another, without a break, for hours.

'Goes on sale' is not the same as 'is drawn'

Here's the mix-up worth untangling. When someone asks 'what time does the Christmas Lottery start', sometimes they mean the sale and sometimes the draw, and they are two very different things. The sale of décimos (tenth-share tickets) begins in summer (in late June) and stays open until 21 December, the eve. The draw, on the other hand, is a single moment: 22 December at 9:00.

Put another way: you can buy your décimo over almost half a year, but it's all played out in that one morning of the 22nd. If you were after the time to buy, relax — you've got months; if you were after the time of the draw, it's 9:00 on the dot.

How long does it last and what time does it finish?

 

The draw lasts around four hours and usually finishes at about 13:00, though some years it stretches to 13:30. It has no fixed finishing time because it depends on how long it takes to complete every extraction: the draw isn't over until all 1,807 prizes have been sung out and the prize drum is left empty.

And why so many prizes? Because the Christmas draw doesn't hand out only the big ones — El Gordo, the second, the third — but thousands of smaller prizes too: it's the famous pedrea, along with the approximations, the hundreds, the endings and the reintegros (refunds). Every one of them has to be sung out one by one, with its two balls and the approval of the panel, and there lies the secret of why the morning goes on so long: it isn't that the draw is slow, it's that it gives out an enormous amount.

How the draw works on the inside: drums, boards and breaks

 

The draw goes on for a long time because it hands out a huge number of prizes, and doing it with every safeguard in place takes time. Understanding how it works on the inside explains why it lasts a whole morning and why there are breaks. Let's look at the parts.

The two drums

The draw uses two drums. The large one holds 100,000 balls, one for every possible number, from 00000 to 99999. The small one, called the prize drum, holds 1,807 balls, as many as there are prizes to hand out. The San Ildefonso schoolchildren draw one ball from each drum at the same time: one gives the number and the other the prize that goes with it.

Among those 1,807 prize balls is everything from El Gordo to the humblest ball of the pedrea, by way of the second and third prizes. And here's an important detail: every ball weighs the same and every one has exactly the same chance of pairing up with your number. There are no 'better' balls, no luckier positions, no order that can be predicted. Each extraction is independent of the one before, just as in any other draw.

The boards and the breaks

Each pair of balls that is sung out is threaded onto a wire, and those wires are grouped onto boards. When a board gathers 200 balls of each kind, it is closed and swapped for another: that is the reason for the short breaks you see during the draw. On top of that, the children take it in turns through the morning. Neither the breaks nor the board changes mean the draw truly stops: they are part of the rhythm of a very long morning.

El Gordo can come out at any moment. There is no 'El Gordo hour': it can drop in the first few minutes or near the very end of the draw. So, if you're following the draw, it's best not to switch off until the prize drum is empty.

And the official count (escrutinio)? When the result becomes official

 

Every prize in the Christmas draw is validated on the spot: a panel checks each extraction before the ball is threaded on, so the result is confirmed as the morning goes along. When the draw ends, Loterías publishes the full official list of prizes, which is the one that counts for all purposes.

In practice, this means you can trust the prizes as they're sung out almost instantly, but to claim a prize — or to write off a décimo — the sensible thing is to check against the official list once the draw has closed. And there's no rush: remember that Christmas Lottery prizes can be claimed for three months, so there's no need to hurry on the 22nd itself.

It's worth being clear, too, about the difference between the provisional and the official on such an intense day. With the draw under way, a thousand screenshots do the rounds, half-finished lists and messages saying 'I think you've won'. That's all lovely for riding the excitement, but to make any decision — to celebrate, to claim or to discard a décimo — what counts is the official list, not a screenshot. Checking calmly against the final list saves you the classic scare of trusting a figure that hadn't yet been confirmed, something especially easy to fall for amid the avalanche of information on draw day.

Is there a number to win the lottery today, in 2026?

No, not at Christmas either. Décimos come with their number already printed and they all have the same probability of winning a prize: there are no 'lucky' numbers and no particular hour at which El Gordo is more likely to come out. It can drop in the first minute or near the end, and any number has the same chances. Pick the one that appeals to you and play responsibly; anyone selling you 'today's winning number' is deceiving you.

How does the 1220 code work for winning?

It doesn't. The '1220 code' (or '1220 method') is numerology that circulates on social media and promises to deduce the next number from the last result. It's false: the Christmas draw, like any other, is pure chance, and no calculation based on past results can predict which ball will come out. What's more, décimos are pre-printed: there is nothing to 'calculate'. Enjoy the draw for what it is — a bit of hope and excitement — and don't waste money chasing methods that don't exist.
