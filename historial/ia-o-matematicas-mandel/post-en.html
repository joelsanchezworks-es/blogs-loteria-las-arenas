The short answer

Mandel didn't guess the numbers: he bought them all

Stefan Mandel's ‘trick’ wasn't predicting the winning combination, but buying every possible combination in a draw when the jackpot was so big that it was worth it. It's not guesswork or artificial intelligence: it's combinatorics and economics. And it only works under very specific conditions that today almost never occur.

Who was Stefan Mandel and what exactly did he do

 

Stefan Mandel was a Romanian economist who started playing out of sheer necessity: he wanted to raise enough money to emigrate from communist Romania. He landed his first two wins there, in the sixties, with a method he called ‘combinatorial condensation’: a system for reducing the number of tickets needed while still covering a huge number of combinations.

Over time, by then settled in Australia, he refined the idea into its crudest and most effective version: if a rolled-over jackpot was greater than the cost of buying every possible combination, then buying them all stopped being madness and became an investment with a practically guaranteed profit. You don't need to get it right: if you hold every ticket, one of them is necessarily the winner.

That's how he strung together 14 jackpots across Romania, the United Kingdom and Australia. All perfectly legal. He wasn't cheating or ‘hacking’ anything: he simply exploited a situation in which the maths swung in his favour. The key was patience: waiting for the specific draw where the numbers added up.

For his plan to make sense, four conditions had to be met at the same time. And here's the detail almost no one mentions when they romanticise his story:

→ The jackpot had to exceed the cost of buying every combination (counting the smaller prizes that came in as a knock-on effect too).

→ Few possible combinations: it was only viable in lotteries with few numbers, where buying everything was physically possible.

→ Permissive rules: you needed to be able to buy (or print) tickets in bulk, something that was allowed back then.

→ Little risk of sharing the jackpot: if another winner turned up and it had to be split, the sums fell apart.

The Virginia 1992 coup: the exact figures

 

His most famous play was the Virginia Lottery (United States) in 1992. There you chose 6 numbers from 1 to 44, which gave ‘only’ 7,059,052 possible combinations. When the jackpot rolled over to $27 million, Mandel saw his chance: buying every combination cost around $7 million, well below the prize.

He set up a fund with thousands of investors, hired a team that spent months printing the tickets needed to cover the seven million combinations and submitted them en masse. On 15 February 1992 the numbers 8, 11, 13, 15, 19 and 20 came up… and, of course, one of his combinations had them. He took the $27 million jackpot, plus hundreds of thousands in smaller prizes.

Looked at coldly, it wasn't luck: it was arithmetic. Here are the figures that explain everything:

Why doesn't the method work any more in modern draws?

 

Here's the key point that dismantles every imitator. Mandel's method relied on a very fragile balance, and modern draws have broken it completely. The main reason is size: the more numbers a draw has, the more combinations there are, and the more impossible (and more expensive) it becomes to buy them all.

Compare 1992's Virginia with today's EuroMillions and you'll see it at once. In EuroMillions you choose 5 numbers from 1 to 50 plus 2 Lucky Stars from 1 to 12, which rockets the total to 139,838,160 combinations. At €2.50 each, buying them all would cost around €350 million… more than the jackpot cap of €250 million. In other words: even if you won the maximum jackpot, you'd lose money.

And that's without even counting the logistical impossibility: no one can buy 140 million tickets within a draw's sales window, the rules forbid it, and if by some miracle you managed it, any other winner would force you to split the prize. The method doesn't ‘fail’: it's that the conditions that made it viable no longer exist.

And what about artificial intelligence? Can it predict the numbers?

 

If Mandel won with maths, plenty of people wonder whether AI, which is maths on steroids, could go a step further and ‘predict’ the combination. The answer is a resounding no, and it's worth understanding why, because it isn't an opinion: it's how chance works.

An AI learns from patterns. It works wonderfully when there's a hidden regularity to uncover: the weather, language, traffic. But a lottery draw is designed, precisely, to have no pattern. Each draw is independent of the last: the balls don't remember what came up last Saturday. However much historical data you feed an algorithm, there's nothing to learn, because there's nothing to predict.

Let's separate what AI can do from what it can't, so as not to fall into the snake-oil sellers' trap:

✓Calculate probabilities and tell you, precisely, how unlikely each combination is.

✓Spot biases in a faulty machine (something exceptional that gets corrected immediately).

✓Organise a syndicate or hand out combinations without repeats, just as an advanced spreadsheet would.

✗Predict the next combination: impossible, because each draw is independent and random.

✗Find ‘hot’ numbers that are due to come up: the past doesn't influence the future in a fair draw.

The big misunderstanding

Neither AI predicts nor did Mandel predict: the only ‘edge’ was buying everything

Put the two ideas together and you'll see the misunderstanding at its root: no one has ever predicted a lottery combination, neither with formulas nor with AI. What Mandel did wasn't prediction, it was full coverage with the sums in his favour. The moment you can't buy everything at a profit, there's no ‘system’ left worth anything: you're back to exactly the same probability as any player with a single ticket.

One customer's miscalculation: the ‘wealth loop’ that wouldn't close

 

It happened with a razor-sharp customer of ours, an engineer, one of those people who love numbers. He read Mandel's story and came in thrilled: he'd ‘understood the system’ and planned to apply it to EuroMillions. His plan was what he proudly called his ‘wealth loop’: start by buying lots of combinations, reinvest the small prizes that came in to buy even more, and thereby edge closer and closer to Mandel's full coverage until the loop closed on its own.

The flaw was in the very first line of his reasoning, and it's a textbook miscalculation. Mandel didn't buy ‘lots of’ combinations: he bought them all, 100%. Our customer, playing hard, managed to cover about five hundred combinations per draw. It sounds like an enormous amount… until you set it against the 139 million that exist: he was covering 0.00036% of the total. Statistically, buying five hundred or buying a single one is almost the same: the probability barely moves.

That's where the ‘wealth loop’ turned into a spending loop. Since the big prizes never came (and there was no reason they should), there was nothing to reinvest; and the handful of small prizes that turned up now and then didn't come anywhere near covering what he spent each week. The loop wouldn't close because, mathematically, it couldn't close: it started from confusing ‘buying more’ with ‘buying everything’, and between those two things lies a chasm of 139 million combinations.

We talked it through calmly, with the earlier table in front of us, and he got it straight away — he was an engineer, after all. He swapped the ‘system’ for what genuinely makes sense: playing what you decide to spend, for the pleasure of playing, and not a euro more. Ever since, he plays his EuroMillions with a clear head and, above all, without the anguish of chasing an impossible loop.

The lesson about the nature of chance

 

Mandel's story is fascinating, but the moral isn't ‘there's a trick’. It's exactly the opposite. If the only method that has ever worked in history consisted of buying every combination — and today that's impossible and ruinous — the conclusion is clear: there's no shortcut. Here are the three ideas worth taking home:

→ Chance has no memory: no combination is ‘closer’ to coming up because of what happened before. Every draw starts from scratch.

→ Buying more isn't buying everything: only 100% coverage guarantees a prize, and in modern draws it's neither feasible nor worth it.

→ The only sensible plan is to play with your head: decide on a budget, enjoy the thrill and don't chase formulas that promise the impossible.

Put another way: the lottery is a dream, not an investment. The day someone sells you a ‘method’ for winning guaranteed, remember Mandel: it took buying seven million tickets to ‘secure’ a prize, and today not even that would be enough. The rest is a fairy tale.

Frequently asked questions

 
What is the strategy of the mathematician who won the lottery 14 times?

It's the one used by Stefan Mandel, a Romanian-Australian economist. His strategy wasn't to predict numbers, but to buy every possible combination in a draw when the rolled-over jackpot exceeded the cost of buying them. With every ticket in hand, one of them was necessarily the winner. He pulled it off 14 times, always legally, taking advantage of lotteries with few combinations and rules that allowed bulk buying. There was no magic and no guesswork: it was combinatorics and economics.

Can Stefan Mandel's method be repeated today?

In practice, no. Modern draws have vastly more combinations (EuroMillions, almost 140 million), so buying them all would cost more than the maximum jackpot itself: you'd lose money. On top of that, lotteries changed their rules after Mandel's exploits to prevent the bulk buying and printing of tickets, and there's always the risk of having to share the jackpot. The method relied on conditions that no longer exist.

Can artificial intelligence predict the lottery numbers?

No. An AI learns from patterns, and a fair draw is designed to have none: each draw is independent and random, the balls don't remember what came up before. AI can calculate probabilities, help organise a syndicate or detect a faulty machine, but it can't guess the next combination. Anyone selling you an AI that ‘predicts’ the lottery is selling you snake oil.

So, are ‘hot’ or ‘cold’ numbers any use?

For entertainment, yes; for winning, no. Whether a number has come up a lot or a little in the past doesn't change its probability in the next draw, because each draw is independent. It's what statisticians call the gambler's fallacy. Playing on hunches or on dates is just as valid — and just as unlikely — as any other combination: pick the one that appeals to you, but knowing that none has an edge.
