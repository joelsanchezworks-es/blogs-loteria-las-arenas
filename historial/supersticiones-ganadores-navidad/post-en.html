The quick answer

Rituals are tradition, not a lever over chance

Rubbing the décimo, keeping a San Pancracio, stepping in with the right foot, buying where a prize once fell or playing 'your' special number are the most popular superstitions, and they all have one thing in common: they do not change the odds one bit. The winning number is decided by pure chance and every décimo has the same shot, one in 100,000, no matter where you rub it. Enjoy them for what they are —tradition and shared excitement—, but do not mistake them for a strategy to win.

The most popular superstitions (and where they come from)

 

Most Christmas Lottery rituals are after the same thing: feeling that you are 'helping' luck along, even though that is impossible. They are gestures that bring peace of mind and build tradition, repeated generation after generation. These are the most widespread, grouped by when they are done.

Rituals with the décimo

The all-time classic is rubbing the décimo against something or someone that 'brings luck': a bald person's head, a pregnant woman's belly, a black cat's back or, commonly, the figurine or mascot of the lottery office where you buy it. Some people also kiss it, blow on it or keep it somewhere special: inside the Bible, next to a 'bait' banknote or, above all, beside a San Pancracio, the saint that tradition credits with luck and work. For it to 'work', they say, the figure must be a gift, face toward the inside of the house and always carry its sprig of parsley. None of this alters a number that already comes printed from the factory, but as a ritual it is one of the best loved.

Rituals when buying

The moment of buying has its own choreography. Some people insist on stepping into the lottery office with a particular foot —and here the superstition contradicts itself, because some swear it is the right foot and others the left— and on taking the décimo with the right hand. Some choose the left or right queue depending on whether the day is even or odd, have a child touch or choose the number, or only buy where the Gordo (top prize) once fell in another year. And, of course, there are the meaningful numbers: a date of birth, an anniversary, the car's licence plate. They are all charming gestures, but none of them improves odds that are identical for any number and at any counter.

Why do we get so superstitious about the lottery?

 

Superstition grows exactly where two ingredients come together: a lot at stake and no control over the outcome, and the Christmas Lottery is the perfect storm of both. When something matters enormously and we can do nothing to influence it, the mind looks for a way to regain a sense of control, even if it is illusory. Rubbing the décimo or setting out a San Pancracio are, deep down, ways of soothing that helplessness in the face of chance: they do not change the draw, but they do change how we feel while we wait.

Besides, almost all the rituals are inherited and shared: a grandmother taught them to us, the whole family repeats them, we see them on TV every year. That makes them as much a part of Christmas as turrón (Spanish nougat), so they endure generation after generation despite having no basis. And there is no harm in it: they are harmless as long as they do not lead you to spend more than you should 'just in case', or to mistake a lovely tradition for a strategy that does not exist. Enjoying the ritual while knowing it is a ritual is the healthiest way to live it.

Counter archaeology: the strangest rituals we have seen

 

From behind the counter you see everything, and every December we confirm that the imagination for 'summoning luck' knows no limits. With all the affection in the world, here are some of the most curious rituals we have witnessed over the years.

Some, the moment they pay, rub the décimo against the figurine by the entrance before tucking it away, like someone charging a battery. Some bring a child by the hand with the single mission of touching the number 'so it brings more luck'. Some buy and, right there, take a photo of the décimo with whichever saint is on hand in the background. And some, in all seriousness, ask us which number 'has the best energy', to which we always give the same answer: every one of them exactly the same.

Our favourite, year after year, is the ritual of burning the previous draw's décimos with the flame of a candle of a particular colour, 'to renew your luck'. And the most practical, without a doubt, is the customer who strides in firmly on one foot and walks out, without noticing, slipping the décimo into a pocket with a hole in it. That is the only ritual that can truly cost you the prize: which is why we insist so much on safe custody.

The curious thing is that almost no one performs a single ritual: they stack up. The same person rubs the décimo, steps in with the obligatory foot, shows it to their saint and picks a number using their grandchild's birth date, all in the same visit. And we are delighted: as long as the excitement does not turn into spending more than planned, a little Christmas theatre does no one any harm. The only thing we ask, half-joking, half-serious, is that after so much ritual they do not leave the décimo forgotten on the counter… because we have seen that too.

The reality of the real winners

 

If there is one thing we have learned from watching prizes land, it is that the real winners have no ritual in common: the only thing they share is that they had a décimo. There is no 'lucky person' profile, no magic gesture that repeats among the winners. Some played their lifelong number and others bought on impulse; some rubbed the décimo and others did not even think of it.

What many sad stories do share is the opposite: prizes that were nearly lost to a mislaid décimo, to not checking properly or to not claiming it in time. No superstition is any use there; there are very real, very avoidable slip-ups. That is why, when someone asks us the secret of the winners, the honest answer is boring but true: have a décimo, look after it and check it. The rest is chance.

Frequently asked questions about superstitions and luck

 
What superstitions do Spanish Christmas Lottery winners have?

The most popular are rubbing the décimo (against a bald person's head, a belly, the lottery office mascot…), keeping a San Pancracio with its sprig of parsley, going in to buy with a particular foot and taking the décimo with the right hand, choosing meaningful numbers (dates, licence plates) or buying where the Gordo once fell. They are endearing traditions, but it is worth knowing that none of them changes the odds: every décimo has the same shot, one in 100,000.

Does rubbing the décimo or buying where a prize once fell do any good?

No. Rubbing the décimo does not alter a number that is already printed, and buying 'where it once fell' does not improve anything either: those lottery offices appear more often on the winners' lists simply because they sell far more décimos, not because they win more often. The odds per décimo are identical at every point of sale. They are charming rituals, but their effect on the draw is exactly zero.

Do winners perform any special ritual?

There is none in common. From watching prizes land over the years, the only thing winners share is that they had a décimo: some played their usual number and others bought on impulse, some did rituals and others none at all. There is no 'lucky person profile' and no magic gesture that recurs. Chance does not tell the difference between someone who rubs the décimo and someone who simply puts it away.

What is the only thing that truly helps?

The things that do not depend on luck: buying with guarantees, keeping the décimo in safe custody, checking it in full and claiming it within the deadline. It does not change your odds —nothing does—, but it makes sure that, if luck does arrive, you do not lose it to carelessness. Most prizes that go unclaimed are not lost in the draw drum, but afterwards: to a mislaid décimo or to not checking properly.

Is there a number to win the lottery today in 2026?

There is not, with or without a ritual. Every number has the same probability and there are no 'lucky' figures, charms or formulas that guarantee a prize. A number or a gesture giving you a good feeling is wonderful for the excitement, but it does not change your chances: they are still one in 100,000. Anyone who sells you 'today's winning number' is deceiving you; the winning number is only known once it comes out of the draw drum.

How does the 1220 code work to win?

It does not work. The '1220 code' is numerology that circulates on social media and promises to predict results from previous draws. It is as much a superstition as rubbing the décimo: false. Every draw is pure chance and the décimos are pre-printed, so there is no pattern to 'calculate' and no code to apply. No method can foretell the Gordo. The only useful thing is to buy with guarantees and check your number carefully.
