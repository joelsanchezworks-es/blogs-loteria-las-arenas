The short answer

No system turns a losing bet into a winning one

The lottery has a negative expected value: it pays out only a fraction of what it takes in, so every bet, on average, is worth less than it costs. No betting-management method changes that. The Martingale system needs infinite money and no limits; buying every combination costs more than you can win; and the cases of those who ‘hacked’ the lottery were temporary loopholes, long since closed, not magic formulas. The only sensible ‘method’ is to play a little, for fun, knowing it is a game of chance.

Why no method can ‘beat’ the lottery

 

The key lies in a concept called expected value, or mathematical expectation: on average, every lottery bet returns less money than it costs, and no system can turn that around. It is the same reason the house always wins at the casino, only in the lottery the margin is even bigger.

Lotteries pay out only a portion of what they take in — depending on the game, around half, and a little more in draws such as the Lotería de Navidad (Spanish Christmas Lottery). The rest goes to taxes, to running the operation and to the State’s cut. That difference is the ‘house edge’, and it is a hole through which money leaks on every play, whatever you do with your bets.

The mistake behind every ‘foolproof method’ is believing you can offset that edge by playing in a particular way: doubling up, covering more numbers, following a pattern. But a betting system only rearranges how you lose, not whether you lose. Mathematically, adding up bets with a negative expected value always gives a negative expected value: there is no combination of bad plays that produces a good one.

Here it helps to separate two worlds that people tend to muddle. There are games where skill matters — poker, blackjack with card counting — and in them a very good player can, with years of discipline, turn the house edge around. But the lottery is not one of those games: it is pure chance, with no decisions you can optimise. There is nothing to learn, calculate or train that improves your odds, because the numbers come up at random and each draw is independent of the last. That is why the ‘systems’ that half-work in games of skill make no sense at all applied to a draw: there is no expertise possible against a ball that falls at random.

The Martingale system: doubling your stake until you go broke

 

The Martingale system means doubling your stake every time you lose, on the theory that when you finally win you recover everything lost plus one unit of profit. On paper it looks foolproof; in practice it ruins you. It is a system with more than three centuries of history and it has never worked, for three devastating reasons.

→ It needs infinite money: after a bad run, the stakes grow so fast (2, 4, 8, 16, 32…) that within a few steps they outstrip any real pocket.

→ It needs no limits: every real game has a maximum stake, so a point comes when you can no longer double.

→ Losing streaks are guaranteed: over time, long runs of defeats appear, and just one is enough to sweep away everything won before, and more.

And that is in games with ‘almost 50-50’ odds, such as red/black on the roulette wheel. In the lottery the Martingale is even more absurd, because it is not a double-or-nothing bet: the chances of winning are minuscule and the prizes are tiered. Doubling here gets you no closer to recovering anything; it only makes you lose faster. The Martingale does not beat the house: it just changes the pace at which you hand over your money.

The ones who did ‘win’: loopholes that no longer exist

 

There have indeed been people who won money from the lottery systematically, but not by beating chance: by exploiting specific design flaws that temporarily turned a losing bet into a winning one. And every one of those loopholes is now closed. They are the exception that proves the rule, and they are worth knowing because they illustrate perfectly the difference between ‘getting lucky with a trick’ and ‘running a mathematical operation with a real edge’.

Stefan Mandel: buying every combination

The most famous case is that of the mathematician Stefan Mandel, who won fourteen times by buying every combination… but only in small lotteries and at specific moments when the rolled-over jackpot exceeded the cost of covering them all. It was an arbitrage operation, not a probability trick: he only stepped in when the sums came out in his favour. Even so, it required an army of people filling in tickets by hand, printing presses and enormous logistics, on a margin that almost any hitch could swallow. As soon as the lotteries increased the number of combinations and banned mass ticket printing, the play stopped being possible. Today, in games such as EuroMillions, not even that strategy would add up.

Cash WinFall: the loophole that MIT squeezed dry

The other great example is the American game Cash WinFall, which had a ‘roll-down’ rule: if the jackpot was not won, the money rolled down to the lower prize tiers and multiplied those prizes. A group of students from MIT (and some retirees working on their own) realised that on those particular days each ticket was worth more than it cost, and they bought hundreds of thousands in one go. They won millions over the years… until the lottery spotted the flaw and shut the game down. Once again, the lesson is the same: they did not beat chance, they exploited a design flaw that no longer exists. They did not find a magic number or a system; they found a temporary flaw in the rules, and they acted as investors, not as players.

Be wary of any ‘foolproof method’ someone tries to sell you. If a formula to win the lottery for certain really existed, whoever had it would use it quietly to get rich, not sell it to you over the internet. The only person who wins with those ‘systems’ is the one charging you for them.

So how do you play sensibly?

 

The lottery is entertainment, not an investment: the sensible way to play is to accept that, on average, you lose, and to play for the thrill with a budget you do not mind spending. No serious mathematician would play the lottery expecting to make money in the long run; they might well play, like anyone else, for the excitement of taking part, and with small amounts.

The difference between enjoying the lottery and getting into trouble is precisely there: playing for fun and within a limit, rather than chasing losses with ‘systems’ or spending more and more to ‘win it back’. That chase is the trap that ruins people, and it has nothing to do with luck. If you fancy a flutter, do it sensibly, with a fixed amount set aside for fun and with proper safeguards, and forget magic formulas: they do not exist.

One very simple trick helps you not to cross that line: decide in advance how much you want to spend in a month — what you would spend on any other little treat — and do not go beyond it, win or lose. If a draw does not come up, you do not ‘win it back’ by playing more on the next one; you have simply enjoyed your little moment of hope and that is that. That way the lottery stays what it should be: a small entertainment expense with the remote but real chance that one day it changes your life. No systems, no stress and no chasing anything.

Frequently asked questions about ‘foolproof’ methods

 
Why do mathematicians go broke playing the lottery?

Because the lottery has a negative expected value: on average, every bet returns less than it costs, since the game pays out only a fraction of what it takes in. Anyone who tries to ‘beat’ that with a system — doubling stakes, covering combinations, chasing losses — only manages to lose faster, because adding up bad bets never produces a good one. It is not bad luck: it is the maths of the game itself, and that is why no ‘foolproof method’ works in the long run.

Does the Martingale system work on the lottery?

No. The Martingale system (doubling your stake after every loss) needs two things that do not exist: infinite money and no limits. After a bad run, the stakes grow so fast that they wipe out any budget, and a long run of defeats always arrives in the end and takes the lot. In the lottery it is even worse than at roulette, because it is not a double-or-nothing bet: doubling gets you no closer to recovering anything, it just speeds up the losses.

Can you buy every combination and win for certain?

In practice, no. In EuroMillions there are 139,838,160 combinations: buying them all would cost around €350 million, more than the maximum possible jackpot (€250 million), so you would be certain to lose. In smaller games the sums look tighter, but you would have to share the prize with other winners and it is physically impossible to validate millions of bets within the sales window. It only worked in the past, in specific lotteries with jackpots that exceeded the cost, and those cases are now blocked.

Is it true that someone ‘hacked’ the lottery?

Yes, but not by beating chance — by exploiting design flaws. The mathematician Stefan Mandel won fourteen times by buying every combination when the jackpot exceeded its cost; and a group from MIT squeezed the game Cash WinFall dry during its ‘roll-down’ days, when each ticket was worth more than it cost. They won a great deal of money, but these were arbitrage operations that demanded huge resources, and every one of those loopholes was closed as soon as the lotteries spotted them.

Is there a number to win the lottery today, in 2026?

There is no such thing — neither a number nor a method. Every result is pure chance and there is no formula, system or ‘code’ that guarantees a prize; if there were, whoever had it would not be selling it. The only certainty is that the lottery is a game of negative expectation: you play for fun, not as a strategy to make money. Anyone who promises you ‘today’s winning number’ or a sure-fire method is deceiving you.

How does the 1220 code work for winning?

It does not work. The ‘1220 code’ is numerology that circulates on social media and promises to predict results from previous draws. It is just one more of the ‘foolproof methods’ that fail: each draw is independent and purely random, so there is no pattern to ‘calculate’. No code, system or calculation can guess the result. The only sensible thing is to play with your head, for fun and with proper safeguards.
