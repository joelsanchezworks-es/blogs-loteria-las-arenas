What El Millón is (and why it’s so easy to forget)

 

El Millón is a draw that’s exclusive to Spain, bundled at no extra cost into every EuroMillions bet you make. When your bet is validated, the state lottery system automatically assigns you a code made up of five numbers and four letters. That code goes into its own draw, which hands out €1,000,000 at every EuroMillions session, completely separate from the main jackpot.

You don’t pick it, and you don’t have to do anything special: for every €2.50 bet you play, you get your El Millón code thrown in for free, printed right there on your receipt. It’s literally a second shot at a prize you’ve already paid for — and one that plenty of people never get around to making the most of.

In fact, some El Millón prizes take a while to surface because the lucky winner didn’t even know they were in that draw: they bought with only the EuroMillions jackpot in mind, filed the receipt away and, with the code nowhere in their thoughts, it never occurred to them to check it. Stories like that come up far more often than you’d imagine, and they almost always end well only when someone remembers to look at the code in time.

Here’s the trap: because the code is assigned on its own and printed in a corner of the ticket, it’s very easy to forget it’s even there. Most people check their five EuroMillions numbers and two stars… and completely forget to see whether their El Millón code has come up a winner. They’re really two different draws living on the same ticket:

How El Millón is drawn and what it pays out

 

The way it works is simple. At every EuroMillions draw, El Millón is drawn too: a winning code is pulled with the same format as yours (five numbers and four letters), and whoever holds that exact code takes home €1,000,000. There’s nothing else to match: it doesn’t depend on the numbers you played, only on the code you were handed when you validated your bet.

That has an important upshot for you: you can miss every single EuroMillions number and still be a millionaire thanks to the code. They’re completely independent prizes. Which is why checking only the main combination and forgetting the code is, no exaggeration, leaving half your ticket unread.

And since the code is drawn at every session, the chance that, over time, one of your tickets carries a winning code isn’t some far-off fantasy. The question isn’t only whether you’ll win — it’s whether you’d even find out. That’s exactly where the oversight creeps in.

It’s worth being clear about one thing, though: none of this changes the odds of anything. The code is an added prize, not a trick to win more. It’s simply a part of the ticket you’ve already paid for that, out of habit or of not knowing, far too many people leave unchecked. Making the most of it is free; ignoring it can, sometimes, cost you dearly.

Why so many El Millón prizes go unclaimed

 

It’s not one single reason, but a pile-up of several very common slip-ups. These are the ones that come up most often:

→ Only the numbers get checked: people look at the 5 numbers and 2 stars, and the El Millón code slips right past them.

→ The code is separate: it’s a different draw with its own check; if you don’t go looking for it, it simply won’t show up.

→ A quick scan can fool you: a photo or a phone scan can trip up on a ticket that’s crumpled, folded or worn, and flag ‘no prize’ by mistake.

→ The ticket just gets binned: after seeing ‘no win’ on the numbers, lots of people throw the ticket away without ever looking at the code.

→ The prize expires: after 3 months, an unclaimed prize is gone; time is working against you.

Notice that none of these reasons has anything to do with luck: every one of them is avoidable with a minute of attention. More often than not, the prize isn’t lost in the draw machine — it’s lost on the sofa at home, checking in a rush.

An example we see all the time sums it up nicely. Someone plays their usual EuroMillions, checks the numbers on their phone on Friday night, sees they didn’t match the combination and leaves the ticket forgotten in a wallet. Weeks later they find it crumpled up and, before binning it, bring it in out of curiosity. Checking the El Millón code, it turns out it wasn’t the top prize — but it was a ticket that was well worth a second look. The scene plays out every season: what changes is whether the ticket gets checked in time or ends up in the bin.

The three-month clock: don’t let your prize expire

 

Even if you do check the code, there’s a second silent enemy: time. In Spain, EuroMillions and El Millón prizes come with a three months deadline to claim them, counted from the day after the draw. That’s not long, and it flies by when a ticket gets set aside ‘to look at later’.

When that deadline passes, the prize expires: however much of a winner the ticket was, there’s nothing more to be done, and that money doesn’t go back to the player. It’s the literal definition of a forgotten fortune: real prizes that existed and then evaporated because they weren’t checked and claimed in time.

That’s why the best habit is a simple one: check your tickets as soon as you can — numbers and code — and don’t let ‘pending’ receipts pile up in your wallet or a drawer. EuroMillions has thirteen prize tiers, and the smaller ones — a refund, a couple of stars — are precisely the ones people most often leave uncollected, because ‘it’s only a little’ or they never get around to checking. Added together, a lot of money is left hanging every season. Checking properly is collecting what’s already yours, whether it’s a million or a few euros.

Frequently asked questions

 
What is the El Millón code and where does it come from?

It’s a code of five numbers and four letters that the state lottery system automatically assigns to every EuroMillions bet validated in Spain, at no extra cost. It goes into its own draw, El Millón, which pays out €1,000,000 to one code at every EuroMillions session. You don’t choose it: it’s handed to you when your bet is validated, and it takes part in every draw (Tuesday and Friday) without you having to do anything beyond keeping your receipt.

Why do so many El Millón EuroMillions prizes go unclaimed?

Mostly through carelessness: lots of people check their EuroMillions numbers and stars but forget that the El Millón code is looked at separately. Add to that the quick scans that get misread and the habit of writing off a ticket without ever checking the code. Since the prize expires after 3 months, that one slip can cost a million. The fix isn’t more luck — it’s a minute of attention at the moment you check.

Why does my ticket look like it didn’t win when it actually did?

Almost always because the check was incomplete or unreliable: only the numbers were looked at and not the code, or a phone scan failed on a crumpled or worn ticket. Before writing off a ticket, verify the El Millón code character by character against the official winning code for that draw; don’t trust a single glance. It’s not that the official system gets it wrong — it’s that a home check, done in a hurry, only goes halfway.

How long do I have to claim an El Millón prize?

The deadline is three months from the day after the draw, the same as every other EuroMillions prize. Once that time is up, the prize expires and can no longer be claimed, so it’s best to check your tickets as soon as you can and not leave them forgotten in a drawer for weeks. A simple trick: check on the same weekend as the draw, while you still have the ticket to hand and the number fresh in your memory.
