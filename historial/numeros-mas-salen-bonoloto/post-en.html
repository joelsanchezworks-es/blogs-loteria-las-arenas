The short answer

The frequencies are real statistics about the past, but zero prediction of the future

A number having come up a lot doesn’t make it any more likely in the next draw, and a number that hasn’t appeared for a while isn’t ‘due’ either. Every Bonoloto draw is independent: the balls don’t remember what happened yesterday. Every combination has exactly the same odds, 1 in 13,983,816, whatever statistic you care to look at.

Which numbers come up most in the Bonoloto?

 

Real lists of the most frequent Bonoloto numbers do exist, and they’re easy to look up; what doesn’t exist is any power to predict. Let’s look at them head-on, because understanding where those figures come from is exactly what dismantles the myth.

The real part: yes, some numbers have come up more often

It’s true: if you add up every draw in the history of the Bonoloto — more than 9,000 since 1988 — some numbers appear more often than others. As things stand today, the most frequent is 33, closely followed by 10 and 34. So far, an objective fact: it’s what has happened, and anyone can check it.

But look closely at the figures, because they’re the key. Across more than 9,000 draws, each number has come up on average around 1,140 times; the most frequent, 33, is close to 1,200. In other words, between the number that ‘comes up most’ and the average there’s a gap of barely a few dozen… spread over almost forty years. That tiny difference isn’t an ‘edge’: it’s exactly the noise that pure chance produces.

The myth part: they’re no use for predicting anything

Here comes the false step almost everyone takes: thinking that, because 33 has come up more, it ‘stands a better chance’ of coming up today. It’s false. In the next draw, 33 has exactly the same odds as 7, as 49, or as the least-drawn number in history. The past gives it no edge whatsoever.

Why? Because each draw is independent and the drum has no memory. Frequency statistics describe what has already happened, but have no ability whatsoever to anticipate what will happen. Confusing those two things — describing the past and predicting the future — is the root error behind every ‘system’ built on hot numbers.

‘Hot’ and ‘cold’ numbers: why they’re a mirage

 

‘Hot’ numbers (the ones that have come up most recently) and ‘cold’ ones (the ones that have come up least) are the modern version of an old superstition, and they don’t work. They’re worked out over a recent window — the last 100 draws, say — and promise to spot ‘streaks’. The trouble is that in randomness there are no streaks that carry on and no debts that get paid. Here are the most common myths and the reality behind them:

The idea that a ‘cold’ number must come up soon is called the gambler’s fallacy. It’s one of the most studied errors of reasoning… and one of the most expensive.

Chance has no memory. Every Bonoloto draw starts from scratch: no combination is ‘closer’ to coming up because of what happened before. Every single one, without exception, has the same odds: 1 in 13,983,816.

Why checking the ‘numbers that come up most’ can empty your wallet

 

The real danger of frequency lists isn’t that they don’t work: it’s that they make you play worse. By giving you a false sense of a ‘system’, they push you into spending more than you would if you knew it’s all chance. And that’s where the myth becomes a problem for your wallet.

Think about it: if you believe you’ve found ‘the good numbers’, it’s very easy to slip into playing more bets ‘so as not to waste them’, into switching combination every week chasing the latest streak, or into upping your spend convinced that ‘now it’s due’. None of those decisions improves your odds one bit, but every one of them costs you money. The system doesn’t win; it only spends.

That’s the paradox of these ‘strategies’: they don’t increase what you can win, but they do increase what you can lose. The best combination isn’t the ‘hottest’ one: it’s the one you can comfortably afford to play.

What really matters: responsible play

 

If the statistics don’t help you win, what really makes the difference? How you manage your play. There’s no trick for winning more often, but there is a sensible way to play that protects your wallet and keeps the fun intact. It comes down to these habits:

✗Switching numbers every week chasing the ‘hot’ ones of the moment.

✗Spending more than you planned because you think a number is ‘due’.

✗Buying more bets convinced that a system improves your chances.

✓Setting a budget for your play and not going over it, whatever happens.

✓Playing for fun, as entertainment, never as an investment or a way to make money.

✓Choosing whichever numbers you fancy — a date, your usual ones, at random — knowing they’re all worth exactly the same.

Frequently asked questions

 
Which numbers come up most in the Bonoloto?

Adding up the whole history of the Bonoloto (more than 9,000 draws since 1988), the numbers that have appeared most often are 33, 10 and 34, in that order. That said, the gap with the average is tiny: each number has come up on average around 1,140 times and the most frequent is close to 1,200. That difference is simple chance, not an edge. And, above all, it’s no use for predicting: in the next draw, those numbers have exactly the same odds as any other.

Is there any point in playing the numbers that come up most, or the ‘hot’ ones?

No, for winning it’s no use at all. ‘Hot’ numbers (the ones that have come up most lately) and ‘cold’ ones (the ones that have come up least) start from a mistaken idea: that draws follow streaks. They don’t. Each draw is independent, so a number that has come up a lot isn’t ‘on a streak’, and one that hasn’t come up for a while isn’t ‘due’. You can play them if you fancy — they’re worth the same as any other — but not because they give you any edge. Believing they do is exactly what leads a lot of people to overspend.

So, are the Bonoloto statistics real or a myth?

Both, depending on what for. As a record of the past they’re real: the numbers have come up as many times as they have, and that’s an objective fact. As a prediction tool they’re a myth: they have no ability to anticipate the next draw, because chance has no memory and every combination is equally likely. The error lies in confusing describing what happened with guessing what will happen.

Is there a number to win the lottery today, in 2026?

There isn’t, and anyone who says otherwise is deceiving you. Neither the frequency lists, nor the ‘prediction’ apps, nor any hunch can know which combination will come up today: each draw is pure chance and independent. Every combination has the same odds, the same in 2026 as in any other year. The sensible thing is to choose the numbers that excite you, play only what you decide to spend, and enjoy the game responsibly, without hunting for a ‘winning number’ that simply doesn’t exist.

How does the 1220 code work for winning?

It doesn’t. The ‘1220 code’ (or ‘1220 method’) is numerology doing the rounds on social media and in videos, promising to work out the next number from the last result. It’s the same trap as the ‘hot numbers’, just in different wrapping: it takes for granted that a pattern exists, when there isn’t one. In the Bonoloto, as in any lottery, each draw is independent and random, so no calculation based on past results can predict future ones. Chasing those methods only serves to make you overspend.
