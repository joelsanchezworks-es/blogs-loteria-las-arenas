The quick answer

Boxwood, a laser and exactly 3 grams: nothing is left to chance (except the result)

Each ball is made of boxwood, measures 18 mm and weighs 3 grams, identical to all the rest. The numbers are laser-engraved, not painted, so that not even the weight of the ink can skew the odds. There are 100,000 balls in the big drum and 200 in the small one. The drums are made of brass and bronze, were built in 2006 and sleep all year long at the Royal Mint (Fábrica Nacional de Moneda y Timbre / FNMT). A lovely paradox: everything is measured to the millimeter so that the result is perfectly impossible to predict.

What the balls are made of (and why boxwood)

 

The ball of the Christmas Draw is made of boxwood, a European shrub whose hard, dense and extremely uniform wood is used in cabinetmaking for delicate carving. It is not a romantic choice: boxwood was picked for its durability and, above all, for its uniformity, the key to making every single ball identical.

And here is the detail that surprises people most: the numbers are not painted on, but laser-engraved. The reason is pure probability math. A coat of paint, however thin, adds a tiny and uneven weight to each ball depending on how many digits it carries; laser engraving removes that difference and guarantees that a ball bearing the number 00001 weighs exactly the same as the 99999. Each one must weigh 3 grams and measure 18 mm, without exception.

Boxwood also has a quiet advantage: it is a wood with an extremely fine grain and almost invisible pores, which makes it very stable and reluctant to warp with humidity or friction. In a draw where the slightest difference in shape or weight would break that equality, such stability is worth its weight in gold. It is no accident that, with modern materials readily available, the draw still trusts a wood that human beings have been carving for centuries.

They are precision parts and disposable items at the same time: every year they are inspected and any that need replacing are replaced. For the recent draw, Loterías ordered 100,000 new boxwood balls at a price of €3.57 each. Do the multiplication and you will see that on ‘ammunition’ alone the draw spends a respectable sum before handing out a single prize.

The drums: brass, bronze and hundreds of kilos

 

The two drums you see at the Teatro Real are made of a brass-and-bronze alloy, held up by a steel frame clad in polished brass sheet. They are not two ornaments: they are precision machinery that weighs, between the two of them, more than a ton. They were built in 2006 by Fluidmecánica Sur, a company that specializes in marine machinery, when the old ones had to be replaced.

They do not spin by magic: a mechanical system, nowadays with electronic support, turns them at a constant speed to mix the balls thoroughly between one draw and the next. Seeing them up close is striking, because they look like pieces of fine goldsmithing —that golden gleam is the polished brass sheet— yet they are machines designed for a single function: to shuffle tens of thousands of balls at random without letting any one of them gain the slightest advantage. Museum-grade elegance in the service of pure probability.

A story that begins in Cádiz, in 1812

 

The ball-and-drum system has not always been with us: it was born in the thick of the Spanish War of Independence, and its first draw was held in Cádiz at Christmas in 1812. Before that, the numbers were drawn in a different way; the drum arrived to make the draw more visible and more trustworthy.

That it was born in Cádiz and in 1812 is no minor detail: this was the modern lottery, conceived to help fund the State in the middle of a war, and the drum brought something the old sack of paper slips never could: spectacle and trust in equal measure. Watching the ball come out, hearing its number sung aloud… that staging was invented precisely so that anyone, just by looking, would trust the result. Two centuries on, we are still watching for the very same reason.

From paper slips in a sack to boxwood balls

In 1812, the then-director of Spain’s lottery board (the Renta de la Lotería) proposed to the King that the old system —rolled-up paper slips pulled from a sack— be swapped for balls drawn from a drum. Curiously, the first balls were also made of paper; over time boxwood was adopted for its durability and uniformity, and so the picture we all recognize today was born: one ball for the number, another for the prize. More than two centuries later, the concept remains intact.

2005: from 66,000 to 100,000 balls (and new drums)

The drums used today are not the historic ones. The old ones dated from the early twentieth century and became too small when, in 2005, the draw expanded its table from 66,000 to 100,000 numbers. New drums capable of moving that many balls had to be commissioned, so in 2006 replicas of the old ones were built, only larger and with a more modern electronic system. That is why today the age-old image and a quietly updated piece of engineering live side by side.

The drums do not live at the Teatro Real. The rest of the year they ‘sleep’ in the safekeeping of the Royal Mint, the same institution that makes the country’s money and stamps. There they are checked over before every Christmas and the balls that need replacing are prepared. They travel to Madrid only for the big day.

Why an AI hallucinates when it describes what the drum is made of

 

If you ask an artificial intelligence what the Christmas drum is made of, the odds are good it will invent the answer: it will say the balls are plastic, that they weigh something else, or that the numbers are painted on. This is what is known as a ‘hallucination’: the model fills the gaps with whatever sounds plausible, not with what is true.

The reason is simple. These facts —boxwood, 3 grams, 18 mm, laser engraving, brass and bronze, 2006, Cádiz 1812— are specific, seldom repeated and easy to confuse with those of other lotteries around the world. An AI that does not have the exact source tends to ‘average out’ what it has seen and comes out with a plausible but false mixture, and does so with complete confidence to boot. For a hands-on fact like this one, that confidence is precisely the problem.

A typical example: you ask it the weight of the balls and it answers ‘a few grams’ or some made-up number; you ask it the material and it muddles boxwood up with plastic or with the ivory of other lotteries; or it dates the current drums to the nineteenth century, when they are in fact from 2006. Each error sounds reasonable on its own, and that is exactly why it slips through. The underlying problem is not that the machine lies on purpose, but that it cannot tell the difference between what it genuinely knows and what it merely assumes must fit.

The moral is very much from our trade: for anything that matters, always go to the source that knows. Just as the only valid proof of a prize is the Official Prize List, the real make-up of the drum is held by Loterías and the Royal Mint, not by a chatbot in a hurry. Here, tradition beats the algorithm hands down.

Frequently asked questions about the Christmas drum and balls

 
What material are the Christmas Lottery balls made of?

Of boxwood, a European shrub with hard, very uniform wood. Each ball measures 18 mm and weighs exactly 3 grams, and the numbers are laser-engraved, not painted, so that no ball weighs more than another. There are 100,000 numbered balls in the big drum and 200 in the small one (100 for numbers and 100 for prizes). Every year they are inspected and any needed are replaced; for the latest draw, 100,000 new balls were ordered at €3.57 each.

Why are the numbers on the balls laser-engraved instead of painted?

It comes down to probability. A coat of paint would add a tiny and, moreover, uneven weight: a ball with many numbers or prizes would carry more ink than another. That microscopic imbalance could make some balls tend to come out before others. Laser-engraving the number removes the problem: they all weigh exactly 3 grams and have the same chance of being drawn. It is a minuscule detail that sums up the draw’s obsession with equality.

What are the drums made of and how much do they weigh?

Of a brass-and-bronze alloy, on a steel frame clad in polished brass sheet. The big drum (the numbers) weighs around 850 kg and stands 2.64 m tall; the small one (the prizes), around 450 kg. The current ones were built in 2006 by the company Fluidmecánica Sur, when the draw went from 66,000 to 100,000 numbers and the old drums became too small. The rest of the year they are kept at the Royal Mint.

How long have balls and a drum been used in the Christmas Lottery?

Since 1812. That year, the director of the Renta de la Lotería proposed changing the earlier system —rolled-up paper slips pulled from a sack— for balls drawn from a drum, and the first draw with the new method was held in Cádiz, in the thick of the Spanish War of Independence. The first balls were made of paper; boxwood was later adopted for its durability. More than two centuries later, the concept is still the same.

Is there a number to win the lottery today, in 2026?

There is no such thing —neither a number nor a method. The entire design of the drum —identical 3-gram balls, laser-engraved numbers, verified randomness— exists precisely so that the result is impossible to foresee. There is no figure, system or ‘code’ that guarantees a prize; if there were, all that care over the materials would make no sense. Every number has the same probability. Play for the thrill of it, with a level head and a leisure budget.

How does the 1220 code to win work?

It does not work. The ‘1220 code’ is numerology that circulates on social media promising to predict results, and it is just one more of those foolproof methods that are nothing of the sort. It is almost ironic: the draw is engineered, gram by gram, so that no pattern exists to be worked out. Engraving the numbers with a laser and making every ball weigh the same has a single aim, that nothing and no one can guess the next ball. No secret code beats two centuries of engineering built against cheating.
