People always pick the same lottery number not because it improves their odds —it doesn’t—, but out of fear of regret: the idea that ‘the year I drop it will be the year it wins’ feels unbearable. On top of that, over time that number starts to feel like ‘yours’, and letting it go is experienced as a loss. Pure workings of the mind, not mathematics.

And there is one setting where that anxiety multiplies: your regular bar. When half the regulars have played the same number for years, not buying it one December turns you, in your own head, into ‘the only one who’ll be left out if it wins’. That collective pressure —very Spanish, very much a thing of the bar counter— is one of the reasons almost nobody dares to change.

In this guide we tell you why we replay the same number year after year, what psychological biases lie behind it and why the bar amplifies that feeling. You’ll see you are not alone: it happens to almost everyone, and it has a name and an explanation.

And, above all, we tell you the good news: you don’t have to choose between peace of mind and your usual number. The problem was never the number; it’s playing it badly. You can have both.

The quick answer

We replay the same number out of fear of regret, not strategy

We always pick the same number for three psychological reasons: the fear of regret (imagining it wins the very year you drop it), the endowment effect (once it’s ‘yours’, letting it go hurts) and group pressure (the whole bar plays it). None of them improves your odds: every number has exactly the same chance, one in 100,000. But they explain why changing causes so much distress. The solution isn’t to change or to obsess, but to play your number with confidence, so that fear disappears.

Why we always pick the same number

 

We replay the same number because the human mind would rather avoid a loss than chase a gain, and losing ‘our’ number —or having it win once we no longer hold it— feels exactly like a loss. It isn’t a rational calculation; it’s a well-studied emotional mechanism, and it works the same way all over the world.

The probability, in reality, is indifferent to the number you choose: 00000 has the same odds as 45,678 or as your usual number. But our heads don’t reason with probabilities, they reason with stories and emotions. And there are two that rule over all the rest.

And it isn’t a quirk of yours or a lack of intelligence: it’s how anyone’s mind is built. The same mechanisms that make us replay a number kick in when we hold on to an old object ‘just in case’ or when we struggle to switch bank or phone company. The lottery simply puts them especially on display, because it brings together money, chance and a date loaded with emotion. Knowing that it happens to everyone is the first step towards playing with a calmer mind.

The fear of regret: the driver behind it all

The main driver is regret aversion: we don’t play so much for the thrill of winning as to avoid the bad taste of not having tried. Psychology has measured it: in a well-known study, several people were offered the chance to swap their lottery number for another, and almost all refused, even though the new number had exactly the same odds. The reason? Sheer panic at the thought of their number coming up a winner after they had let it go. That imagined regret weighs far, far more than the real probability, which is minuscule. That’s why ‘the year I drop it will be the year it wins’ is the phrase you hear most at any bar counter.

The number is already ‘yours’: the endowment effect

The second driver is the endowment effect: the moment something becomes ours, we value it more simply for owning it. A number you’ve played for years stops being just any number and turns into ‘your’ number, with its history, its near-misses and its memories. Changing it feels like abandoning something of your own. Often the sunk cost fallacy piles on top —‘I’ve been playing it for ten years, I can’t stop right now’—, as if the accumulated years gave you some claim over the next draw. They don’t: the drum has no memory, and your ten years of loyalty don’t change this year’s odds by so much as a hair.

Your regular bar: when the pressure turns collective

 

At the local bar, the individual fear of changing turns into group pressure: if everyone has played the same number for years, being the only one who doesn’t buy it is experienced as a social risk, not just a financial one. It’s the most Spanish angle of them all, and the one that generates the most anxiety.

It works like a shared prophecy. Everyone pictures the same scene: the number comes up a winner, the bar erupts with joy… and you’re the only one who, for whatever reason, didn’t buy it that year. It’s not so much the money lost as being left out of the collective celebration, watching your friends or colleagues toast a prize you too used to play every year. Psychology sometimes calls this ‘preventive envy’ or the fear of missing out (the famous FOMO): we buy, in part, so as not to be left out.

That’s why, in practice, the bar’s number becomes a kind of unspoken contract: nobody wants to be the one who breaks the chain. And there’s nothing wrong with that —sharing a number is a lovely tradition, it brings people together—, but it’s worth acknowledging that what pushes us isn’t the statistics, it’s not wanting to be ‘the odd one out’ who got left behind. Understanding that already takes away half the pressure.

The good side of the shared number

 

The fact that there is psychology behind the fixed number doesn’t make it a bad thing: the shared number of the bar, the workplace or the family is also one of the loveliest traditions of the Spanish Christmas. It’s worth saying, because this article isn’t about convincing anyone to give up their number, but about understanding why they replay it.

A shared number is, deep down, a small pact of belonging. Playing ‘the bar’s one’ makes you part of something: of a crew, of a neighbourhood, of a routine that repeats every December with the same faces. The value isn’t in the probability —which is the same as that of any other number— but in the ritual: the chat at the counter, the ‘have you got it yet?’, the shared anticipation over weeks. That bond is real and worth a great deal, even though no statistic says so. That’s why the conclusion isn’t ‘stop playing your number’, but ‘play it wisely and without fear’: enjoy the tradition and strip away its one bad part, which is the anxiety of losing it or of it going wrong.

The number you choose isn’t the problem; the real mistake is playing it badly —losing your décimo, not reserving it in time or not organizing the bar syndicate (peña) properly—.

Frequently asked questions about the fixed number

 
Why do people always pick the same lottery number?

Above all out of fear of regret: the idea of the number coming up a winner the very year you drop it feels unbearable, far more than the real probability of it happening, which is tiny. Added to that is the endowment effect (once the number is ‘yours’, letting it go is experienced as a loss) and group pressure, especially when the same number is played at the bar or at work. They are psychological mechanisms, not strategies: replaying a number does not improve the odds.

Do I have better odds if I always play the same number?

No. Always playing the same number doesn’t raise your odds by so much as a hair. Each draw is independent and every number has the same chance, one in 100,000, whether it’s played for the first time or the twentieth. The drum has no memory: it doesn’t ‘reward loyalty’ nor ‘owe’ anything to a number for having gone years without coming up. Playing the same number is a perfectly valid emotional decision, but not a statistical advantage.

Is it bad luck to change your lottery number?

No, just as keeping it doesn’t bring good luck either. These are beliefs, not facts: the result is pure chance and the probability is identical whether you change or not. The feeling that ‘changing brings bad luck’ comes from the fear of regret, not from any reality of the draw. You can change your number with complete peace of mind, or keep yours because it excites you; neither of the two options has any bearing on whether you win.

How do I make sure I don’t lose ‘my’ number each year?

By reserving it in advance at a trusted lottery office (administración). That way you make sure you have ‘yours’ without the last-minute scramble, and that, if it comes up a winner, they let you know and the prize isn’t lost through an oversight.

Is there a number to win the lottery today in 2026?

There isn’t one, neither ‘your’ number nor any other. They all have the same probability and there are no ‘lucky’ figures or formulas for getting it right. A number giving you a good feeling is wonderful for the excitement, but it doesn’t change the odds: they are still one in 100,000. Anyone who sells you ‘the winning number’ is deceiving you; the winner is only known once it comes out of the drum.

How does the 1220 code work to win?

It doesn’t work. The ‘1220 code’ is numerology that circulates on social media and promises to predict results from previous draws. It’s false: every draw is pure chance and the décimos are pre-printed, so there is no pattern to ‘calculate’, nor any ‘lucky’ number to replay. No method can foretell the Gordo (top prize). The only sensible thing is to play whatever number you fancy, with guarantees and without anxiety.
