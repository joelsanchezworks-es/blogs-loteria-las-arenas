What exactly is 'El Gordo'?

 

'El Gordo' is simply the name everyone uses for the first prize of the Spanish Christmas Lottery; officially it's the 'first prize', and it's the one that pays out the most money per décimo. It isn't a separate draw or a special prize: it's the highest category in the very same draw held on 22 December.

The nickname comes from the fact that it's by far the 'fattest' prize of the year — 'gordo' means fat — and no other Spanish National Lottery draw pays out this much per décimo. When someone asks 'what is El Gordo?', the answer has two parts: on one hand it's a category (the first prize, €400,000 per décimo) and, on the other, it's a specific number, the one drawn as the first prize that day. Until the draw takes place, 'El Gordo' is the category; afterwards, it's also that number we all remember.

One thing is worth being clear about: the Gordo pays €400,000 to each décimo, not a single four-million prize for one lucky person. Because each number is sold across many series, and each series has ten décimos, the same Gordo is usually shared among a lot of people. That's why we say it 'shares out': it's rarely collected by a single person.

El Gordo is also the most eagerly awaited prize of the year: the hunt for it starts weeks before 22 December and fills lottery offices all over the country. That anticipation is part of the tradition, but it's worth keeping it separate from the numbers: dreaming is free, and knowing exactly how much each category pays helps you play with a clear head and clear sums, without getting carried away by the buzz of those days.

Décimo, series, fraction and full ticket: how a prize is measured

 

To understand the amounts you need to be clear on four terms, because a prize can be quoted per décimo or per series, and the difference is tenfold. It's the most common source of confusion, and clearing it up saves you a lot of misunderstandings.

There's a fifth term worth knowing too: the share (participación). It isn't the same as a décimo. A share is a small slice of a décimo — for example, €5 of a €20 décimo — that's split among several people, usually with a small donation added on for the body that issues them (a social club, a bar, an association). If the décimo wins, each share collects its proportional part. It's the most common way to play as a group with little money.

The décimo (which is also the fraction)

The décimo is what most people buy: it costs €20 and is one tenth of a full ticket. In the Spanish Christmas Lottery, the décimo is exactly what's technically called a fraction: each full ticket is divided into ten fractions, and each fraction is a décimo. So when you see 'prize per décimo' or 'per fraction', it's the same figure. The prizes announced 'per décimo' (€400,000 for El Gordo) are what someone holding one décimo of that number collects.

The series and the full ticket

A series is the complete set of the ten décimos of a single number; that set is also called a full ticket, and it costs €200 (ten décimos of €20). The 'per series' prizes are ten times those per décimo: if El Gordo gives €400,000 per décimo, it gives €4,000,000 per series. Each number is issued in many series, and that's the reason a single winning number can pay out in many places at once.

Beyond El Gordo: approximations, hundreds and endings

 

Beyond the 'big' prizes, the draw pays out several smaller categories that mean a great many décimos that 'didn't win' actually do have a prize. They're the ones most often overlooked when checking, and they're worth knowing:

→ Approximations: the numbers immediately before and after El Gordo collect €2,000 per décimo; those of the second prize, €1,250; and those of the third, €960.

→ Hundreds: the 99 numbers in the same hundred as the 1st, 2nd or 3rd prize (that is, sharing the first three digits) collect €100 per décimo.

→ The last two digits (endings): décimos that match the last two digits of the 1st, 2nd or 3rd prize collect €100 per décimo.

→ The pedrea: these are 1,794 prizes of €100 per décimo scattered down the list, the fine drizzle of small prizes that sprinkles across a great many numbers.

The practical upshot matters: a décimo can hold a prize even if it isn't 'the number'. So when you check, don't just look at whether it's El Gordo; also review the approximations, the hundred and the endings, because that's where €100 prizes hide that many people throw away without meaning to.

The refund prize: getting back the €20 you paid for the décimo

 

The refund prize is the smallest and most frequent prize: it rewards décimos whose last digit matches that of El Gordo, and it consists of getting back the €20 the décimo cost. It's the category that pays out to the most numbers — there can be up to 9,999 numbers with a refund prize in each draw — and, while it won't make you rich, it has an obvious upside: it gives you back what you staked, so 'you get to play again for free'.

A detail that surprises a lot of people: if your number matches El Gordo on the last two digits, you collect the €100 ending and, because the final digit matches too, the €20 refund prize as well; €120 per décimo in total. That's why we insist so much on checking right down to the last digit: the refund prize is the one thrown in the bin most often simply because nobody looked.

Frequently asked questions about El Gordo and the Christmas Lottery prizes

 
What is El Gordo in the Spanish Christmas Lottery?

'El Gordo' is the popular name for the first prize of the Spanish Christmas Lottery, the category that pays out the most money per décimo. It isn't a separate draw: it's the highest prize in the draw held on 22 December. Before the draw, 'El Gordo' refers to the category (the first prize); afterwards, it's also used for the specific number drawn as the first prize that year.

How much is El Gordo in the Spanish Christmas Lottery per décimo?

El Gordo pays €400,000 per décimo (the décimo costs €20) and €4,000,000 per series, which is the ten décimos of a single number. It's the largest amount in the whole draw. Bear in mind that, on prizes of this size, a 20% tax applies to the portion above €40,000, so the net per décimo for El Gordo is €328,000.

How much do the second and third Christmas Lottery prizes pay?

The second prize pays €125,000 per décimo (€1,250,000 per series) and the third prize, €50,000 per décimo (€500,000 per series). Below them are the two fourth prizes (€20,000 per décimo each) and the eight fifth prizes (€6,000 per décimo each). All of them belong to the same draw held on 22 December.

What is the pedrea and how much does it pay?

The pedrea is the draw's many small prizes: 1,794 prizes of €100 per décimo spread all down the list. It's called that because it falls like a fine drizzle that sprinkles across a great many numbers. Although €100 per décimo won't change your life, it's five times what the décimo cost (€20), so it's worth checking carefully in case your number is in the pedrea.

Is there a number to win the lottery today in 2026?

There isn't one, not even at Christmas. Every number has the same probability of being El Gordo: there are no 'lucky' figures and no formulas to get it right. What is real is understanding how each prize is shared out — which is what we explain here — so you know what you can win with your décimo. Anyone selling you 'today's winning number' is deceiving you; the winning number is only known once it comes out of the drum.

How does the 1220 code work to win?

It doesn't. The '1220 code' is numerology that circulates on social media and promises to predict the result from past draws. It's false: the Christmas Lottery draw is pure chance, the décimos are pre-printed, and there's no pattern to 'calculate' to guess El Gordo. The only thing that's useful and free is to know the prize categories well and check your whole décimo, right down to the refund prize.
