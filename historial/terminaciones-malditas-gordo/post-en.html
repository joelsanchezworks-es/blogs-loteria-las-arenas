Yes, there are endings that have never won a prize in the Christmas Gordo: there are fifteen two-digit combinations that have never matched the last two digits of the top prize. But they are neither cursed nor any more likely to come up for having spent so long ‘pending’. They are a lovely statistical curiosity, not a tip for picking a winner.

The fact grips us because it touches a very human nerve: the urge to look for patterns where there is only chance. In the queues at lottery offices, the same argument breaks out every December: ‘don’t buy that number, it ends in a cursed one’, versus ‘buy it precisely for that reason, it’s due by now’. Both positions sound logical and both are, mathematically, a mistake.

In this piece of counter archaeology we go over which endings have never come up, which ones have made the most history and why so many figures are left ‘unused’ without anything strange behind it. This is Angle 3 (historical and local): the little legend of the cursed numbers, told with the data in hand.

And it is worth starting by taking apart a headline you will see every December: ‘the endings you should buy to win the Gordo’. It is pure clickbait. No statistic from the past can tell you which number to buy, for a very simple reason we will explain: the draw does not remember what it did the year before.

The quick answer

The ‘cursed’ endings exist as a fact, not as a curse

There are fifteen two-digit endings that have never been the ending of the Gordo. It is not magic: with far fewer editions of the draw than possible combinations, it is mathematically normal that a good handful of endings still have not appeared. Every number has the same probability — one in 100,000 — whatever history may say. Avoiding those endings or buying them ‘because they are due’ is the same mistake: the famous gambler’s fallacy.

The 15 endings that have never come up in the Gordo

 

These are the fifteen two-digit endings that, to this day, have never matched the last two digits of the Christmas top prize: 09, 10, 21, 25, 31, 34, 41, 42, 43, 51, 54, 59, 67, 78 and 82. The list is consistent across the main statistical compilations of the draw.

One nuance is worth clarifying: we are talking about the last two digits, not the thousands (the first two digits), which is a different statistic altogether. And the list is updated: the 2025 Gordo was 79432, ending in 32, an ending that was not among the fifteen, so the list stayed the same. That is the charm of this fact: every December it can cross one off the list… or none at all.

The fact that an ending has gone more than two centuries without coming up is impressive, but it means nothing for the next draw. The drum (el bombo) has no memory: it does not know that 09 is ‘running late’, nor does it owe anyone anything.

And why fifteen, and not ten or twenty? It is pure chance. Over the years, the list will normally keep getting shorter as new endings fall, but with no fixed rhythm: there will be years when none is crossed off and others when two go at once. The same happens with the thousands — the first two digits —: there are also opening blocks that, according to SELAE, have never headed a top prize. The map of ‘what is still to come up’ is enormous, and it will stay that way for generations.

The digits that do make history

 

At the other extreme are the digits that have crowned the Gordo most often. And here the number 5 rules, by a wide margin. As the mathematician Claudi Alsina sums it up, 5 has been the last digit of the top prize on 32 occasions, well ahead of the rest. Here are the headline figures:

Among the two-digit endings, 85 is king with 7 appearances, followed by 57 with six; a rung below sit 64 and 75, and depending on the source 65, 90 or 97 also sneak in (here the lists no longer quite agree). These are delicious facts for after-dinner conversation, but — careful — they do not turn 5 or 85 into numbers ‘with an edge’: they still have exactly the same probability as the rest. History tells us what happened, not what will happen.

Why are there endings that ‘never’ come up?

 

The answer lies not in luck or in any curse, but in a simple question of sample size: there are far fewer editions of the draw than possible combinations. Let us take it apart calmly, because it is the key to everything.

It is not magic: it is a small sample

Two-digit endings run from 00 to 99: a hundred possibilities. But the Gordo has only been drawn a limited number of times in its history. With considerably fewer draws than possible endings, it is mathematically expected that many combinations still have not appeared, just as if you roll a hundred-sided die a couple of hundred times: some faces will not come up and others will repeat. It is not an anomaly; it is exactly what chance predicts. What would be truly strange is if, with so few throws, all one hundred endings had already come up.

It is the same phenomenon as the famous ‘birthday paradox’: in a class of thirty students it is extremely easy for two to share a date, while a great many days of the calendar are left with no one. Chance never deals things out in a neat row, one after another; it deals in clumps, with repetitions here and gaps there. The ‘cursed’ endings are nothing more than those gaps, seen through a magnifying glass and given the name of a legend.

The gambler’s trap: neither ‘cursed’ nor ‘due’

Here is the mistake made by both camps at the counter. Someone who avoids a ‘cursed’ ending thinks they have fewer chances; someone who buys it thinks it is ‘due’ for having gone so long without coming up. Both fall into the gambler’s fallacy: believing that past results influence the next one. They do not. The drum keeps no tally of what has come up; every December, the 100,000 balls start from zero and all of them, whatever their ending, are worth exactly the same. No ending is being punished, and no other is owed a prize.

It is the same trap that leads people to look for ‘their lucky number’ or to replay last year’s ticket ‘just in case it comes up now that I’ve let it go’. These are endearing rituals and there is nothing wrong with them as long as they are lived as what they are: customs, not strategies. The problem only appears when someone spends more than they should, convinced that an ending gives them an edge. That edge, quite simply, does not exist.

No ending gives you an edge, for better or for worse. Buy the number that excites you — your birthday, the one you like, the one that ‘speaks to you’ — because they all have the same probability of being the Gordo: one in 100,000. Choosing by superstition is fine for the fun of it; just remember that it does not change your chances.

13, ‘ugly’ numbers and other quirks

 

Beyond the endings, the world of the lottery is full of quirks about numbers, and almost all of them are a matter of aesthetics, not probability. Some people only want ‘pretty’ numbers — palindromes (capicúas) like 12321, low figures, round numbers — while others steer clear of the ‘ugly’ ones: those with a 13 in them, those that ‘don’t sound right’ or those that repeat too many identical digits.

The reality is the same as ever: the drum could not care less whether a number is pretty or ugly. 00000, which so many people find it impossible to imagine coming up, has the same probability as any other. Those preferences do influence one thing: which numbers sell out first at the lottery offices, because we all want the same ones. But not which one wins. Buying for aesthetic taste is part of the party; mistaking it for an edge is, once again, the same mirage.

The counter war: avoid them or buy them

 

The best thing about the ‘cursed’ endings is not the numbers: it is the debate they spark. Behind a lottery counter, this fact is ammunition for endless arguments, and every customer has their own theory.

There are those who, religiously, ask for ‘something ending in 5, the one that comes up most’, convinced they are playing with an edge. There are those who steer clear of 13 or of the endings that have never fallen, just in case. And there is the opposite camp, those who seek out precisely those ‘pending’ endings because, they say, ‘they have to come up some year’. Everyone defends their system with the confidence of someone who has never looked probability straight in the eye.

The honest truth, the one any seasoned lottery seller would tell you, is that this debate decides nothing: it is part of the fun of Christmas, like the turrón or the TV advert. Choosing an ending is a lovely ritual; believing it gives you an edge is a mirage. And as long as one is not mistaken for the other, let the debate be welcome.

Frequently asked questions about the Gordo’s endings

 
Which endings have never come up in the Christmas Lottery Gordo?

There are fifteen two-digit endings that, to this day, have never matched the last two digits of the Gordo: 09, 10, 21, 25, 31, 34, 41, 42, 43, 51, 54, 59, 67, 78 and 82. It is a curious historical fact, but not a tip: every number has the same probability of coming up, one in 100,000, no matter how long it has gone without appearing. The list refers to the last two digits of the top prize, not to the thousands.

Is any ending more likely to be the Gordo?

No. In the history of the draw, the last digit that has come up most is 5 (32 times), followed by 4 and 6 (27 each), and the least, 1 (8 times). But that only reflects what has already happened, not what is going to happen: for the next draw, every digit has exactly the same probability. The fact that 5 has come up more often does not make it a number ‘with an edge’; it is pure accumulated coincidence.

Why are there endings that have never come up?

Because of a small sample. There are a hundred possible two-digit endings (from 00 to 99), but the Gordo has been drawn far fewer times than that. With fewer draws than combinations, it is mathematically normal that many endings still have not appeared, just as rolling a hundred-sided die only a few times leaves faces that do not come up. There is nothing ‘cursed’ about it: it is exactly what you should expect from chance.

Is it better to buy or to avoid the ‘cursed’ endings?

It makes no difference: neither one changes your odds. Avoiding them (as ‘cursed’) or buying them (because they are ‘due’) are the two sides of the gambler’s fallacy, the mistaken belief that the past influences what comes next. The drum has no memory. Buy the number that excites you, whatever its ending: they are all worth the same and all have a one-in-100,000 probability of being the Gordo.

Is there a number to win the lottery right now in 2026?

There isn’t one — neither a number nor a method — and the ‘cursed’ endings are the best example of why. The draw is pure chance: no digit comes up with an edge, neither the ones that have appeared most nor the ones that have gone centuries without doing so. There is no system or ‘code’ that guarantees a prize; if there were, no one would sell it. Every number has the same probability. Play for the thrill of it, sensibly and with a leisure budget.

How does the 1220 code work for winning?

It doesn’t. The ‘1220 code’ is numerology that circulates on social media promising to predict results, and it is a close cousin of the endings myth: it looks for a pattern where there is only chance. Each draw is independent, so there is no digit, code or streak you can ‘calculate’. The only certainty is that every ending, and every number, has an identical probability. Be wary of anyone who promises you otherwise.
