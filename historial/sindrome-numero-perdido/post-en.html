The ‘lost-number syndrome’ is the panic that a décimo you had the chance to buy — usually the one your work colleagues were sharing — and decided to turn down will end up winning. It's not a medical diagnosis or a real disorder: it's a colloquial way of naming a very recognisable fear, a mix of FOMO (the fear of missing out) and anticipated regret. But even if it appears in no manual, anyone who has felt it knows the knot in your stomach is real.

The scene is always similar: the office number goes round, nearly everyone joins in, and you — for money, in a hurry, or just not in the mood that day — say no. From then on, every time the draw comes up, the same film plays in your head: it wins, the group celebrates, and you are the only one left out.

The quick answer

It's FOMO and regret, not a flaw in you (nor a strategy)

The ‘lost-number syndrome’ is the fear of regretting it if the décimo you turned down wins, made worse because it was shared: you wouldn't just lose money, you'd be left out of a collective celebration. It's a normal psychological mechanism — it happens to almost everyone — but two things are worth knowing: turning it down doesn't change the odds (still one in 100,000) and buying out of panic isn't the answer either. The healthy thing is to decide by your budget and your appetite to play, and, if you join in, to do it properly.

What is the ‘lost-number syndrome’?

 

It's the colloquial name for the anticipated panic of imagining that a number you turned down turns out to be a winner; not an illness, but a way of describing an emotion very common at this time of year. Let's be clear from the outset: don't look for ‘the lost-number syndrome’ in any psychology manual, because you won't find it. What you will find are the mechanisms behind it, and those are very real.

At heart, two fears combined. The first is FOMO, the fear of missing out on what others will experience. The second is anticipated regret: we suffer in advance, imagining how we'd feel if the worst happens. When the décimo belongs to a group — the office, the family, a circle of friends — both go off at once, because the prize wouldn't be only financial but social: a shared joy you'd have excluded yourself from.

That's why the syndrome bites harder with the work décimo than with one you'd see in the shop window and not buy. Letting an anonymous number go by isn't the same as saying ‘no’ to something your colleagues will have. At that point it's no longer just the lottery: it's belonging.

And one detail makes it even more uncomfortable: at the office, your ‘no’ is public. Not a private decision you make at home, but an answer you give in front of the group, often with the person collecting the money waiting. That exposure adds a layer of pressure that doesn't exist when you decide alone: you don't just fear the draw's result, you also fear how your ‘no’ will look if things go well for everyone else.

Why turning down the office décimo hurts so much

 

Turning down the work décimo hurts more than passing on any other lottery because it combines two things the mind handles terribly: the fear of social exclusion and regret over an opportunity we let slip. Neither weakness nor greed; it's how we're wired.

It's not the money: it's being left out of the celebration

What really frightens you isn't losing the prize so much as missing the scene: the whole team hugging, heading out to celebrate, and you watching from outside with a forced smile. The human brain fears exclusion from the group almost as much as a physical threat, so it replays that image over and over. In an office, too, that moment would have witnesses and be remembered for years (‘remember the year so-and-so didn't join in?’). It's not the money: it's the fear of being labelled ‘the one who wouldn't’.

Regret over what you didn't do (inaction regret) lasts longer

There's a nuance psychology has studied that explains why this fear sticks: over time, we regret the opportunities we let pass more than the things we did. Money you spent that turned out badly is soon forgotten; an ‘I could have joined in and didn't’ can haunt you for years. Turning down the décimo is, precisely, one of those non-decisions we most fear will become lasting regret. Your mind knows it, so it sets off the alarm ahead of time.

The real emotions at the counter

 

From the other side of the counter we see this syndrome live every December, and it almost always follows the same script. First comes the person who says no with confidence; days later they return, a little embarrassed, to ask whether any of ‘that number’ is left. It's the most repeated scene of the campaign.

Then there's the person who buys the group's décimo with relief, not excitement: not because they want to, but to get the fear off their back. And the person who, in the end, doesn't join in and needs to hear they've done nothing foolish: that the odds were tiny and it's fine. Part of our job, though it may not look like it, is exactly that — bringing the panic down and helping people decide calmly, not on impulse.

There's a third very typical character: the office ‘envoy’, who comes to buy the group's décimo with a list of names on their phone and the duty of leaving no one out. That's who's most grateful when we help put everything in writing, because they know that, if it wins and there's a muddle over who was in and who wasn't, the party can turn against them. You can see in their face they don't want to be the one who, without meaning to, leaves a colleague out.

What we've learned watching so many people come through is simple: almost no one regrets joining in with a small amount and a cool head, and almost everyone who buys out of pure panic feels a little silly afterwards. The emotion at the counter doesn't lie: the problem is never the number, it's deciding out of fear.

A trick that works: picture the draw already over and the number without a prize, which is what will almost certainly happen. If that image leaves you relieved not to have spent the money, your ‘no’ is genuine. If even so you'd have liked to take part for the shared moment, join in — but with a small amount and for the right reasons. Either way, you'll have decided for yourself, not that knot in your stomach that only looks at the worst possible outcome.

Frequently asked questions about the lost number

 
What is the lost-number syndrome?

It's the colloquial name for the fear that a décimo you had the chance to buy and turned down will win, usually the shared one at work or in a group. Not a medical diagnosis or a disorder: a way of describing a mix of FOMO (the fear of missing out) and anticipated regret. The knot in your stomach is real, but remember that turning the number down does nothing to change the odds of the draw.

Is it normal to fear that a décimo I didn't buy will win?

Completely normal: it happens to a great many people, especially with a colleagues' décimo. The mind fears two things at once — exclusion from the group and regret over a lost opportunity — and both go off when the number is shared. Recognising that it's an ordinary psychological mechanism, not a premonition, helps lower the anxiety and lets you decide with a cooler head.

Should I buy the office décimo just in case?

Only if you want to and can afford it, never out of panic. Buying ‘just in case’ to calm the fear isn't playing, it's paying to reassure yourself, and the odds are the same whether you join or not: one in 100,000. If taking part with your colleagues excites you, join in with an amount you don't mind spending; if not, remember that in the vast majority of years that number doesn't win.

How do you properly organise a shared décimo at work?

By putting in writing who takes part and in what share before the draw, and keeping the décimo safe — or the receipt (resguardo) — in the syndicate's name. That way no one is left out through a misunderstanding and, if it wins, the split is automatic and free of arguments. A photo in the messaging group is no good for claiming a prize: what counts is the physical décimo or its official safekeeping. At the lottery office we organise workplace syndicates precisely to avoid these messes.

Is there a number to win the lottery today in 2026?

There isn't one — not the one you turned down, nor any other. Every number has the same probability, and there are no ‘lucky’ figures or formulas for getting it right. Being afraid of losing a number doesn't make it any likelier: its chances are the same as any other's, one in 100,000. Anyone who sells you ‘the winning number’ is deceiving you; the winner is known only once it leaves the drum.

How does the 1220 code work to win?

It doesn't. The ‘1220 code’ is numerology circulating on social media that promises to predict results from previous draws. It's false: every draw is pure chance and the décimos are pre-printed, so there's no pattern to ‘calculate’ and no number to ‘recover’. No method can predict the Gordo (top prize). The only sensible thing is to decide without fear whether you join in, and to play with guarantees if you do.
