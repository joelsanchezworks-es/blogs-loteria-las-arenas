The row that saves the most heartache is the tax one: without co-owners identified, a shared prize can end up paying more than it should.

The underlying problem with the photo is that it mixes up two things that aren’t the same: having an image of the décimo and having a right to a share of the prize. Anyone you forward the photo to has the first; the second is something you have to be able to prove. It’s worth remembering, too, that a décimo is a bearer instrument: the prize is collected by whoever presents the physical ticket, not by whoever keeps the screenshot on their phone. And a chat — with its ‘count me ins’, its voice notes and its deleted messages — is slippery ground if the day ever comes when you have to prove who put in what.

On top of this comes the tax question, which is very real. Lottery prizes are exempt up to €40,000, and the rest is taxed at 20%, withheld when you collect. If a shared prize is collected by one single person in their own name, the tax office sees it as theirs: the exemption is applied once and, when that person shares the money out with everyone else, it can be treated as a gift and taxed all over again. A double hit, just for not putting things on record.

Digital custody cuts that problem off at the root. Because all the co-owners and their shares are on record from the start, each one is treated as receiving their own portion when the prize is collected: each person applies their own exemption, and the payout isn’t a gift. That’s what ‘shielding’ the prize really means — it’s not magic, it’s tying everything down neatly before luck comes knocking.

Sharing on WhatsApp will still be handy for saying ‘shall we play together this year?’. Perfect for that. But when the moment comes for that ‘yes’ to actually mean something — in an argument or in front of the tax office — the photo falls short, and it’s the digital custody that stands up.

The most common mistakes when sharing lottery on WhatsApp

 

None of them come from bad faith: it’s almost always over-confidence. But when there’s a prize on the line, trust isn’t enough. These are the slip-ups we see most:

⚠ Thinking the photo is proof

An image of the décimo proves the number exists, not that you own a share of it. Anyone it gets forwarded to would have ‘the same proof’ as you.

⚠ Pinning everything on an ‘OK’ in the group

A stray message with no amounts or percentages doesn’t say who put in what. And chats get deleted, edited and reinterpreted — they’re not a document.

⚠ Not putting the amounts in writing

‘Let’s go halves’ or ‘put me down for 10’ in a voice note isn’t enough. Without the shares clearly set out and signed, splitting a big prize gets enormously complicated.

⚠ Letting one person collect it in their own name

This is the mistake that triggers the trouble with the tax office: the exemption is applied once, and the later payout can be taxed as a gift. You have to identify everyone when the prize is collected.

⚠ Keeping the décimo in a drawer at home

If the physical ticket is in one person’s hands, everyone else depends on their word (and on it not getting lost or damaged). Held in custody at the lottery office, it belongs to the group.

⚠ Forgetting that the décimo is ‘a bearer instrument’

The prize is collected by whoever presents the physical décimo, not by whoever has the photo. A screenshot gives you no right to collect anything, and passing the image around or leaving the ticket in the wrong hands is an added risk. Official custody avoids exactly that.

Frequently asked questions

 
Does a WhatsApp photo of the décimo work as proof that I’m playing a share?

Not reliably. The photo proves the number exists, but not that you own a share, how much you put in, or what percentage is yours. As evidence it’s weak and easy to dispute; what does work is a signed document listing the participants and their shares, like a digital custody.

What are the legal risks of sharing the lottery on WhatsApp?

Mainly two: a dispute between the participants (who put in what and who gets what, hard to prove from a chat) and a problem with the tax office if one person collects it all, because the later payout can be taxed as a gift. Both are avoided by putting things on record in writing.

Why can a shared prize be taxed twice?

Prizes are exempt up to €40,000 and the rest is taxed at 20%. If a single person collects it, that exemption is applied once and the prize is attributed to them; when they share it out with the others, that money can be treated as a gift and pay another tax. By identifying every co-owner when the prize is collected, each one applies their own exemption and the payout isn’t a gift.
