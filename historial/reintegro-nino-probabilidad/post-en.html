What the refund is (and why it’s called that)

 

The refund is the prize that returns the cost of your ticket — €20 — when its last digit matches one of the draw’s winning digits. It is not a prize in the sense of winning money: it is recovering what you staked. Hence the name: it ‘reintegrates’ your bet.

It is, by far, the most common prize in any big draw, because it hangs on a single digit: the final one of your number. As there are ten possible endings (0 to 9), each refund digit covers exactly 10% of all tickets. At Christmas one ending wins; in El Niño, three. That seemingly small difference is what sends your odds of walking away unscathed soaring.

There is an important nuance many people miss: the refund is paid on top of any other prize your number wins. If your ticket lands a bigger prize and, on top of that, its ending matches a refund digit, you collect both. That is why the refund is not just ‘the consolation prize’: it is a safety net running through the whole draw, and in El Niño that net is three times as dense.

How El Niño’s three refunds are drawn

 

El Niño does not hand out its three refunds all at once: one comes ‘as standard’ with the first prize and the other two are drawn separately. Here is how it works, step by step:

1

The first refund: the tail of the first prize

The last digit of the number that wins the first prize is, automatically, the first refund. If the El Niño top prize ends in 3, every ticket ending in 3 gets the €20 back.

2

Two more balls from the schoolchildren of San Ildefonso

Next, two more balls are drawn from a drum of digits (0 to 9). Each one sets one of the draw’s other two refunds.

3

Three digits, three chances

In the end there are three endings that win a refund. In 2026, for example, they were 3, 1 and 0: any ticket ending in 3, 1 or 0 got its stake back.

The real odds of getting your ticket’s cost back

 

With three different refund digits, the odds of getting your €20 back are 3 in 10 — a clean 30%. Each digit covers 10% of the tickets, and because the three endings are different, they add up without overlapping: 10% + 10% + 10% = 30%. Against the 10% at Christmas, El Niño cleanly triples your chances of not losing out.

A note for anyone hoping to ‘pick a good one’: you cannot improve those odds by choosing a ‘better’ number. For the refund only the last digit counts, and all ten endings (0 to 9) have exactly the same chance of being drawn. Whatever number you buy, you start with three of ten endings in your favour. There is no ‘luckier’ ticket than any other: there are, quite simply, more winning digits than at Christmas.

Getting your money back is not winning. The refund returns your €20: you end up square, neither up nor down on that bet. It is good news — far better than walking away empty-handed — but don’t mistake it for a prize that makes you richer. That distinction is the key to playing with a cool head.

Does it make sense to reinvest your Christmas refund in El Niño?

 

Yes, it makes strategic sense — not as a way to win money, but as a way to play smart. The idea is simple: you reinvest money you had already written off and put it exactly where it has the best chance of coming back. It is a very Spanish tradition — ‘luck that comes back gets played again’ — and, properly understood, it is one of the most sensible calls a player can make. Not because it improves your numbers in the long run (it doesn’t), but because it brings order to how you spend your entertainment budget.

The reasons to say yes

→ You’re playing with ‘found money’: the Christmas refund is €20 you had already written off as festive spending. Reinvesting it does not raise your real gambling budget.

→ You put it where it comes back most: in El Niño, the chance of a refund again is 30%, triple that at Christmas. If you are going to reinvest, it is the draw with the best refund odds.

→ You stretch the fun without overspending: you extend the lottery season to Three Kings’ Day with a bet that, in practice, has cost you no new money.

→ You keep the discipline: the key is to reinvest only the refund, not to add more. That way the game stays controlled leisure and not a snowball.

The honest sum, with numbers

Picture it in euros. You put €20 into Christmas: you have a 10% chance of it coming back as a refund. If it does, you reinvest that €20 in El Niño, where the chance of it coming back again is 30%. You are not multiplying your money — at best you recover €20 and win nothing — but you are squeezing the most out of every euro recovered: you reinvest it at no new cost and in the draw that pays back most. That is the whole ‘strategy’: no magic, no system, just managing your entertainment budget.

By some industry estimates, around 30% of those who win something at Christmas reinvest that €20 in El Niño. It is not a formula for getting rich — there isn’t one — but a tidy way to keep the game going: with money already recovered, in the most ‘giving’ draw of the calendar, and without touching anything you hadn’t planned to spend.

The honest reckoning: why it’s still entertainment

 

However much El Niño triples the refunds, the golden rule does not change: the draw has a negative expected value. As with any lottery, for every euro staked less than a euro is paid back out in prizes; the rest goes on tax and the share the State keeps. Better refund odds do not mean playing with an edge; they simply mean losing a little less often now and then.

It is worth being clear about this, to avoid the ‘I nearly won’ trap: getting the refund one year neither raises nor lowers the chance of getting it the next. Each draw starts from zero, independent of the last. The refund is frequent, yes, but it is still pure chance: it does not build up, it is not owed, and it follows no pattern you can ‘exploit’.

So the healthy approach is the usual one: play El Niño for the thrill of Three Kings’ Day, with an entertainment budget you don’t mind spending, and treat the refund as what it is — getting your stake back — not as a savings plan. The refund ‘strategy’ is not about winning; it is about enjoying the game without it costing you dear.

Frequently asked questions about the El Niño refund

 
What are the odds of getting the cost of a ticket back in the El Niño lottery?

Around 30% — that is, 3 in 10 tickets get at least the refund, which returns the €20 a ticket costs. That is triple the Christmas Lottery, where the refund odds are 10%. The reason is that El Niño draws three refund digits instead of one: as each digit covers 10% of the tickets and all three are different, they add up to 30%. Even so, the likeliest outcome (70%) is still no refund.

How are El Niño’s three refunds chosen?

The first refund is the last digit of the number that wins the first prize. The other two are drawn separately: the schoolchildren of San Ildefonso draw two balls from a drum holding the digits 0 to 9, and each one fixes one more refund. That leaves three winning endings (in 2026 they were 3, 1 and 0). Any ticket whose last digit matches one of the three gets the €20 staked back.

Is it worth reinvesting the Christmas refund in El Niño?

As a leisure strategy, yes, it makes sense; as a way to win money, no (that doesn’t exist). The logic is twofold: you reinvest around €20 you had already written off at Christmas, so you don’t raise your real gambling budget; and you put it into El Niño, the draw with the best refund odds (30% against the 10% at Christmas). It is a tidy way to stretch the fun to Three Kings’ Day. The key is to reinvest only the refund and add no more money.

Is there a number to win the lottery today, in 2026?

There isn’t one — no number and no method. Every draw, El Niño included, is pure chance: there is no number, system or ‘code’ that guarantees a prize, and anyone who had one wouldn’t sell it. How does the ‘1220 code’ to win work?

It doesn’t. The ‘1220 code’ is numerology doing the rounds on social media, promising to predict results — just one more of those foolproof methods that aren’t. Every draw is independent and random, El Niño included, so there is no pattern to work out and no code to crack it. El Niño’s only real ‘mathematical’ edge is its three refunds, and that is in the rules, in plain sight, not in any secret code.
